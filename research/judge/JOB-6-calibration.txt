METRIC 1: EXPECTED CALIBRATION ERROR (ECE), MCE, AND RELIABILITY DIAGRAMS
Definition. Guo et al. split predictions into M equal-width confidence bins and take a weighted average of the absolute gap between accuracy and mean confidence per bin. ECE "approximates" the expected gap "by partitioning predictions into M equally-spaced bins ... taking a weighted average of the bins' accuracy/confidence difference", i.e. sum over bins of (bin size / n) x absolute(acc - conf). They use M = 15 bins. MCE is the worst single bin: "MCE is the largest calibration gap (red bars) across all bins, whereas ECE is a weighted average of all gaps." Reliability diagram: "These diagrams plot expected sample accuracy as a function of confidence. If the model is perfectly calibrated ... the diagram should plot the identity function." Two limits stated in the same paper: "reliability diagrams do not display the proportion of samples in a given bin, and thus cannot be used to estimate how many samples are calibrated", and (supplement) "MCE seems very sensitive to the binning scheme and is less suited for small test sets."
Mechanic for the requester: ECE needs a graded set (confidence plus a right/wrong label per item). It works from text alone if the agent states a number; no logprobs needed. With a few hundred items and 15 bins, many bins hold a handful of items, so read MCE and per-bin gaps as noise (my inference from the quote above, not a measured result).
Guo's headline finding is about vision and text classifiers with softmax outputs, not chat LLMs: "most datasets and models experience some degree of miscalibration, with ECE typically between 4 to 10%."
SOURCE: Guo, Pleiss, Sun, Weinberger 2017, "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks", https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599 (fetched PDF, Sections 2 and S3).
COSTS: a graded set of items with stated confidence; arithmetic only; no logprobs; no GPU.

METRIC 2: ECE IS UNSTABLE UNDER BINNING CHOICES (NEGATIVE RESULT ON THE METRIC)
Nixon et al. tested ECE variants on MNIST, Fashion MNIST, CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet. Quote: "we find that conclusions on the rank ordering of recalibration methods is drastically impacted by the choice of calibration measure." On bin count: "a larger number of bins causes more granular measures of calibration error (low bias) but also a high variance of each bin's measurement as bins become sparsely populated." On cancellation: "Metrics that depend on static binning schemes like ECE suffer from issues where you can get near 0 calibration error due to overconfident and underconfident predictions overlapping in the same bin." Their recommendation: "Adaptive binning schemes lead to more stablity [sic] of metric rank ordering when the number of bins vary, and is also recommended" (equal-mass bins, called ACE). Hsiao 2026 (AECE) and the LLM-judge paper by Tian et al. 2025 (ACE) both report adaptive-bin variants for LLM confidence.
Mechanic: if an agent's stated confidences pile up at 90 to 100 (they do, see the Xiong section below), equal-width bins put nearly everything in one or two bins and ECE reduces to one gap. Report equal-mass-bin ECE together with the count per bin, or switch to Brier (next section).
Caveat: Nixon studied image classifiers, not LLM stated confidence. The bin-sparsity point carries over by arithmetic; the rank-order result is only shown for the vision datasets.
SOURCE: Nixon, Dusenberry, Jerfel, Nguyen, Liu, Zhang, Tran 2019, "Measuring Calibration in Deep Learning", https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01685 (fetched PDF, abstract and Section 3).
COSTS: graded set; arithmetic only; no logprobs.

METRIC 3: BRIER SCORE, AUROC OF CONFIDENCE VS CORRECTNESS, AND RISK-COVERAGE (DISCRIMINATION METRICS)
Calibration and discrimination are different things and need different metrics. Tian et al. 2023: ECE "completely fails to capture the confidences' discriminative power. [Footnote: For binary classification, a system that guesses randomly and outputs 50% confidence each time has perfect ECE.]" They add Brier score ("a proper scoring rule ... that is the mean squared error between the confidences and the correctness labels") and the AUC of selective accuracy against coverage. Kadavath et al.: AUROC "captures discriminative power but is indifferent to calibration (note chance AUROC is 0.5)"; Brier "combine[s] the discriminative power of self-evaluation with calibration (note the chance Brier score on binary choice tasks is 0.25)". Kapoor et al.: AUROC of the confidence is "a setting typically called selective prediction". Lin et al. 2022 note: "a model can be perfectly calibrated ... and not have a MSE of zero. The MSE combines calibration error with 'sharpness'."
Selective prediction curve (Geifman and El-Yaniv): "the entire performance profile ... can be specified by its risk-coverage curve, deﬁned to be risk as a function of coverage." Kamath et al. read it off as "Cov @ Acc=80%": the fraction of questions answered while the answered ones stay 80% accurate.
Mechanic: run all three on the same graded set. AUROC = probability a random correct item gets higher confidence than a random wrong item. All are text-only if the agent states a number or ranking.
SOURCE: Tian et al. 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975; Kadavath et al. 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221; Kapoor et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08391; Geifman and El-Yaniv 2017, https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500; Kamath, Jia, Liang 2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09462 (all fetched).
COSTS: graded set; no logprobs; no GPU.

LOW ECE CAN COEXIST WITH ZERO USEFUL SIGNAL (NEGATIVE RESULT)
Xiong et al. report that prompting tricks reduced ECE without improving the model's ability to separate right from wrong. Quote: "with the CoT prompting on the GSM8K dataset, GPT-4 with 93.6% accuracy achieves a near-optimal ECE 0.064 by assigning 100% confidence to all samples. However, since all samples receive the same confidence, it is challenging to distinguish between correct and incorrect samples." And: "a reduction in overconfidence is due to the diminished gap between average confidence and accuracy, not necessarily indicating a substantial increase in the model's ability to judge the correctness of its responses." Their practitioner warning: "The reliability of the given confidence must be assessed by considering multiple metrics, such as both ECE and AUROC."
Mechanic for the requester: a judge that states "95" every time on a task it solves 94% of the time scores near-perfect ECE, and its AUROC is 0.5 because every item ties (my arithmetic, not a quoted result). Any calibration gate must include (a) AUROC or Brier and (b) the spread of stated values (a count of distinct values used). Hsiao 2026 reports a "Spread" metric, defined as the Bhattacharyya coefficient between the confidence histogram and a uniform reference, alongside calibration error.
Recent abstract-only claim (body not read): "The Mirage of Calibrated Confidence" (arXiv 2609.18453) states "existing metrics like ECE and AUROC cannot detect" that verbalized confidence is insensitive to what the reasoning trajectory contains. It was tested on vision-language models only.
SOURCE: Xiong et al. 2024, ICLR, "Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?", https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063 (fetched PDF, Sections 5.2 and App. D); abstract of https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18453 (fetched via export.arxiv.org API); Hsiao 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10996.
COSTS: graded set; text only.

DOES STATED CONFIDENCE CORRELATE WITH CORRECTNESS AT ALL? (XIONG ET AL. NUMBERS)
No Pearson or Spearman coefficient is reported in the fetched papers; the reported measures are ECE and AUROC (see NOT FOUND list). Xiong et al. Table 2, vanilla verbalized confidence, averages over 8 datasets (values x100): ECE: GPT-3 52.0, Vicuna 46.1, LLaMA 2 43.6, GPT-3.5 37.7, GPT-4 18.0. AUROC: GPT-3 51.3, Vicuna 52.5, LLaMA 2 56.4, GPT-3.5 55.1, GPT-4 62.7. GPT-4's per-dataset AUROC runs from 52.0 (Object Counting) to 81.0 (GSM8K). Their reading: "While GPT-4 displays lower ECE, its AUROC and AUPRC-Negative scores remain suboptimal, with an average AUROC of merely 62.7%--close to the 50% random guess threshold." Also: "the verbalized confidence values predominantly fall within the 80% to 100% range and are typically in multiples of 5."
So: for GPT-3.5 level models the verbalized number is barely above a coin flip at ranking right vs wrong (AUROC 55.1); GPT-4 is better but modest. Scale helps: "approximate 22.2% improvement in AUROC from GPT-3 to GPT-4".
Limits: models tested were GPT-3, Vicuna, LLaMA 2, GPT-3.5, GPT-4 (2023). Newer models are covered only by the Hsiao 2026 section below. Tasks were single-answer QA with a unique ground truth; the authors list open-ended tasks as untested.
SOURCE: Xiong et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063 (fetched PDF, Table 2 and Sections 1, 5.1, 5.2).
COSTS: black-box; no logprobs; one call per item plus a graded set.

VERBALIZED CONFIDENCE VS TOKEN PROBABILITIES ON RLHF CHAT MODELS (TIAN ET AL. 2023)
Claim: for RLHF chat models, asking for a number gave lower ECE than the model's own token probabilities. Quote: "For RLHF-LMs such as ChatGPT, GPT-4, and Claude, we find that verbalized confidences emitted as output tokens are typically better-calibrated than the model's conditional probabilities on the TriviaQA, SciQ, and TruthfulQA benchmarks, often reducing the expected calibration error by a relative 50%."
Numbers, gpt-3.5-turbo, Table 1 (ECE; label-probability vs "Verb. 1S top-1", one-stage verbalized): TriviaQA 0.140 vs 0.068; SciQ 0.256 vs 0.234; TruthfulQA 0.451 vs 0.389. gpt-4, Table 2: TriviaQA 0.078 vs 0.024; SciQ 0.219 vs 0.201; TruthfulQA 0.445 vs 0.350.
Not uniform: on ranking, verbalized was sometimes worse. gpt-4 TriviaQA AUC: label prob 0.950, verbalized top-1 0.937. gpt-3.5 SciQ AUC: 0.752 vs 0.744. For Llama-2-70b-chat (Table 5) TriviaQA AUC fell from 0.865 to 0.793. The authors say the Llama-2 improvement is "much less consistent" than for the GPT and Claude models (Table 5 caption), and "Claude 1 is much weaker at verbalizing its confidence."
So: the win is mainly lower calibration error, not better right/wrong ranking, and it depends on the model family. Verbalized needs only text; the comparison arm needs logprobs.
SOURCE: Tian, Mitchell, Zhou, Sharma, Rafailov, Yao, Finn, Manning 2023, "Just Ask for Calibration", https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975 (fetched PDF, Tables 1, 2, 5).
COSTS: verbalized arm: chat model plus graded set, no logprobs. Baseline arm: logprobs.

RLHF DEGRADES TOKEN-PROBABILITY CALIBRATION; A TEMPERATURE FIX HELPS (GPT-4 REPORT, KADAVATH, TIAN FIG 2)
GPT-4 technical report: "the pre-trained model is highly calibrated (its predicted confidence in an answer generally matches the probability of being correct). However, after the post-training process, the calibration is reduced (Figure 8)." Figure 8 text lists "ECE: 0.007" beside the pre-train curve and "ECE: 0.074" beside the post-trained ("ppo") curve, on a subset of MMLU using logprobs over the A/B/C/D choices; caption: "The post-training hurts calibration significantly." (I read the two ECE numbers off extracted figure text; the adjacency to the model labels is my reading.)
Kadavath et al. (Anthropic): RLHF policies "naively appear very miscalibrated, which is not surprising, since RL finetuning tends to collapse language model predictions towards behaviors that receive the most reward. However, a simple temperature adjustment (with the same temperature T = 2.5 for all evaluations) largely fixes calibration issues." They hedge: "more intensive RL training might distort calibration in ways that cannot be remedied in this way."
Tian et al. Figure 2 caption: "RLHF generally worsens the calibration of Llama-70B's log probabilities, as measured by ECE (lower is better) or AUC (higher is better)."
Implication: calibration evidence from base models does not transfer to chat/agent models. Verify on the actual model you run. Both fixes above use logprobs; if you do not have them, these results are background only.
SOURCE: OpenAI 2023, "GPT-4 Technical Report", https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774 (Section 5, Fig. 8); Kadavath et al. 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221 (Sec. 3.3); Tian et al. 2023 Fig. 2 (fetched PDFs).
COSTS: logprob access to measure; no GPU for evaluation.

WHITE-BOX VS BLACK-BOX GAP (XIONG ET AL.)
Question: is it worth chasing logprobs? Their answer: only a little. Quote from abstract: "Comparisons with white-box methods indicate that while white-box methods perform better, the gap is narrow, e.g., 0.522 to 0.605 in AUROC." In Appendix B.1: "the performance of nearly all methods across various datasets ranges between 0.5-0.6, signifying a limited capability in distinguishing between correct and incorrect responses." Their reason token probabilities fall short: "Token-likelihood primarily captures the model's uncertainty about the next token, rather than the semantic probability inherent in textual meanings."
Kapoor et al. (open models, LLaMA-2/3, Mistral): for open-ended answers "perplexity is generally not predictive of a language model's correctness in open-ended settings and does not exhibit the same favorable scaling," while for multiple choice "maximum softmax probability performs well and improves with the ability of the base model." So token probabilities help on multiple-choice letter answers and much less on free-form answers.
Reading the three together: logprobs are good on single-token multiple-choice and weak on free text; verbalized numbers are weak at ranking but need no special access. Neither is reliable enough alone on hard professional-knowledge items (Xiong: "all investigated methods struggle in challenging tasks, such as those requiring professional knowledge").
SOURCE: Xiong et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063 (abstract, Sec. 6, App. B.1); Kapoor et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08391 (Sec. 3, Fig. 2) (both fetched).
COSTS: white-box arm needs open weights or logprob API; black-box arm needs only text.

PROMPT TRICKS FOR VERBALIZED CONFIDENCE: WHAT WORKED, WHAT DID NOT
Considering several candidate answers (Tian et al.): asking the model to list top-k guesses with a probability each lowers ECE. gpt-3.5-turbo, ECE, one-stage: SciQ top-1 0.234, top-2 0.132, top-4 0.065; TruthfulQA top-1 0.389, top-2 0.361, top-4 0.203; TriviaQA top-1 0.068, top-4 0.054. Authors: "generating and evaluating multiple hypotheses improves calibration (see Figure 1), similarly to humans".
Chain of thought did not help calibration (Tian): "Chain-of-thought prompting does not improve verbalized calibration." Xiong: CoT raised accuracy but "the confidence output distribution still remains at the range of 80%-100%".
Persona prompts did nothing (Xiong, App. B.2, "You are a confident GPT" vs "You are a cautious GPT"): "assuming different personalities does not significantly affect performance metrics such as accuracy, ECE, and AUROC."
No prompt won consistently (Xiong, five strategies, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4): "there is no single strategy that can consistently outperform the others"; Top-K best on GPT-3.5, Self-Probing most consistent on GPT-4; failure prediction stayed "close to the performance of random guess (AUROC=0.5)."
Prompt wording sensitivity (Tian): "the 1-stage and 2-stage verbalized numerical confidence prompts sometimes differ drastically in the calibration of their confidences." In Table 1, 2-stage top-1 SciQ ECE is 0.340 vs 0.234 one-stage.
One contrary result for explicit overconfidence warnings is in the Hsiao 2026 section below.
SOURCE: Tian et al. 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975 (Table 1, Sec. 3 conclusions 2 and 4, Discussion); Xiong et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063 (Sec. 5.2, App. B.2).
COSTS: text only; k-guess prompts cost more output tokens; graded set to verify per model.

SELF-CONSISTENCY ACROSS SAMPLES AS A CONFIDENCE SIGNAL (WANG; XIONG)
Wang et al. 2022: share of sampled answers that agree with the final answer "is highly correlated with accuracy (Figure 5, over GSM8K). This suggests that one can use self-consistency to provide an uncertainty estimate ... low consistency as an indicator that the model has low confidence." No correlation coefficient or ECE appears in the text (figure only); the main results use 40 sampled paths.
Xiong et al. measured it with 5 samples on GPT-3.5 (Table 3; ECE / AUROC x100, averages over 5 datasets): single-call CoT verbalized 25.0 / 56.4; single-call Top-K 17.8 / 65.2; 5 samples with Self-Random + consistency aggregation 18.7 / 73.0; 5 samples "Misleading" 17.3 / 69.6. GSM8K AUROC: "from 54.8% (akin to random guessing) to 92.7%". Professional law: 52.2 to 65.6. So sampling mainly buys ranking (AUROC), less so ECE: single-call Top-K already scored better average ECE (17.8) than Self-Random (18.7).
Cost and saturation: "the improvement becomes marginal as the number gets larger" (tested M=1 to 13); "computational time ... go linearly with the baseline." Wang: "try a small number of paths (e.g., 5 or 10) ... performance saturates quickly."
Limit: needs a fixed answer set to compare samples ("self-consistency can be applied only to problems where the final answer is from a fixed answer set").
SOURCE: Wang et al. 2022/ICLR 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171 (Sec. 3.2 and 5); Xiong et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063 (Table 3, Sec. 5.3).
COSTS: M model calls per item (5 in Xiong); text only; no logprobs; answer-equality check.

AGGREGATION CHOICE MATTERS, AND ONE CHOICE FAILED BADLY (XIONG TABLE 4, GPT-4)
With 5 samples, how you combine them changes the outcome. GPT-4, Top-K prompt, Self-Random sampling, mean over six datasets (x100): ECE: Consistency 12.0, Avg-Conf 14.8, Pair-Rank 6.9. AUROC: Consistency 66.9, Avg-Conf 66.9, Pair-Rank 67.6. Authors: "Pair-Rank emerges as the superior choice for calibration ... while Avg-Conf stands out for its efficacy in failure prediction."
Per-dataset failure: on GSM8K, Avg-Conf (consistency weighted by verbalized confidence) scored AUROC 41.0 against 84.4 for plain Consistency and 80.3 for Pair-Rank, i.e. worse than chance, the paper does not explain why. Avg-Conf also had the best AUROC in five of six datasets. A method that wins on average can invert on one task type.
The practitioner recommendation: "Top-K prompt + Self-Random sampling + Avg-Conf or Pair-Rank aggregation," with the stated warning "none of these techniques consistently outperform others". "Mean & Var" columns show run-to-run spread of 0.2 to 1.7 points, so I would not interpret differences under about 1 to 2 points (my inference).
Mechanic: always report per-task results, not only the mean, before trusting a combined confidence.
SOURCE: Xiong et al. 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063 (Table 4, Sec. 5.4, Sec. 6) (fetched PDF).
COSTS: 5 calls per item; text only; graded set per task type.

SHOWING THE MODEL ITS OWN SAMPLES; P(TRUE) (KADAVATH ET AL. 2022)
Ask the model to propose an answer, then ask whether it is True. Findings: "Self-evaluations are well-calibrated few-shot, though models aren't as well-calibrated zero-shot." Zero-shot, "P(True) is poorly calibrated, and typically it lies close to 50% for typical samples." Showing 5 samples (T=1) beside the one to judge improves Brier: "20-shot evaluation with comparison samples performs best in every case." Format: list five "brainstormed ideas", then "Possible Answer: ... Is the possible answer: (A) True (B) False". Benefit is weaker for long-form tasks: "models benefit less from this approach on tasks requiring long-form answers (Codex and GSM8k)." Also "Few-shot evaluation is important for obtaining good calibration." Models "find their own samples more plausible", which makes self-evaluation harder than evaluating a third party's answers (their footnote 4 also says models cannot tell their own tokens from supplied ones).
Metric facts: ECE here uses 10 equal-count bins, "also been called the mean absolute deviation calibration error [Lin et al., 2022]".
Requirement: P(True) is the probability of the token "True", so it needs logprobs; models were 52B-scale Anthropic base models. A text-only adaptation (ask for a 0-100 number after seeing 5 samples) is NOT tested in this paper.
SOURCE: Kadavath et al. 2022, "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know", https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221 (Sec. 1.1, 4.1, 4.2) (fetched PDF).
COSTS: logprobs; 5 sampled answers per item; few-shot examples.

ENSEMBLING JUDGES WITH A FUSER (OVERCONFIDENCE IN LLM-AS-A-JUDGE, 2025)
Setting: pairwise judging on JudgeBench, models asked for a decision plus a confidence ("Self-Confidence (SC)"). Overconfidence found: "models like DeepSeek-R1-0528 and GPT-4o cluster predictions at high confidence levels (90-100%) but achieve accuracies well below the ideal calibration line"; "high ECE values in GPT-4o (39.25 in SC ...), Mistral-Nemo (74.22 in SC ...), and GPT-4.1-nano (57.03 in SC ...)" (ECE x100).
Ensemble effect: a "fuser" model reads the decisions and written critiques of several judges. Table 4: majority voting 80.00% accuracy / 10.77 ECE; confidence-weighted voting 80.00 / 10.43; entropy-weighted voting 81.71 / 8.48; LLM-as-a-Fuser with Qwen3-235B-A22B 86.29 / 6.42; the same model alone under SC: 77.43 / 11.78. Not all fusers helped: "GPT-4o the weakest (49.71% Acc, 44.07% ECE)". So ensembling lowered ECE, but the choice of fuser model swung ECE from 6.42 to 44.07.
Caveats: single benchmark, one paper (arXiv v3; peer-review status not checked), and the fuser is a second use of a model rather than independent evidence. The paper also proposes TH-Score; I did not verify that metric independently.
SOURCE: Tian, Han, Chen, Xu, Yang, Xuan, Wang, Liao 2025, "Overconfidence in LLM-as-a-Judge: Diagnosis and Confidence-Driven Solution", https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06225 (fetched PDF, Sec. 3, 5, Table 4).
COSTS: several judge calls per item plus one fuser call; text only; graded set (JudgeBench-style labels).

AGREEMENT BETWEEN JUDGES IS NOT INDEPENDENT EVIDENCE (NEGATIVE RESULT, SQUOOSH 2026)
A vendor technical report on LLM judging of A/B tests, pre-registered, labelled by its authors as exploratory tier for the key result. Claim on panel agreement: "judges differing in model or prompt agree with each other at κ = 0.74–0.88 while each agrees with outcomes at only ~0.2, and a 16-vote panel carries ≈2 effective independent votes." Gate: "at a vote-margin >= 0.6 gate the judge calls 49% of tests and abstains on the rest, and on significant-only labels the called subset reaches kappa = 0.311 [0.025, 0.555]." Held-out confirmation at stricter unanimity "returned kappa = -0.100 [-0.200, 0.000] on an underpowered 33-call cohort." Their lesson: "reproducibility must never be mistaken for validity" (gated calls were identical across runs, 31/31).
Mechanic: agreement across samples of one model measures stability, not truth; a confidence gate built on panel margin must be validated against external labels, with the interval width reported. The held-out estimate (-0.100) had the opposite sign to the exploratory one (0.311), on 33 calls.
Weight: single-organisation, non-peer-reviewed; domain is web A/B tests, not general tasks. Use as a warning, not a rate.
SOURCE: Dooskin and Squoosh Technical Staff, June 2026, "The Judge Knows When It Knows", https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07517 (fetched PDF, abstract).
COSTS: panel of N judge calls per item; external ground-truth labels; text only.

FINE-TUNING A MODEL TO SAY ITS CONFIDENCE (LIN ET AL. 2022), INCLUDING THE FAILURE CASE
Setup: GPT-3 175B fine-tuned on 10k examples (about 100 questions per arithmetic sub-task) with the label = the model's measured accuracy on that sub-task, e.g. "floor(100 x p_T)". Evaluated on held-out task families (training on Add-subtract). Lin et al. Table 1 (MSE and MAD, percent, lower better): Multi-answer set: verbalized 22.0 / 16.4; answer-logit zero-shot 37.4 / 33.7; indirect logit 33.7 / 38.4; constant baseline 34.1 / 31.1. Multiply-divide set: verbalized 15.5 / 19.0; answer-logit 10.4 / 9.4; indirect logit 11.7 / 7.1; constant 15.3 / 8.5.
So under a larger distribution shift (Multiply-divide) the fine-tuned verbalized model was WORSE than the zero-shot logit and no better than the constant baseline (MSE 15.5 vs 15.3; MAD 19.0 vs 8.5). Authors' summary: "the model remains moderately calibrated under a substantial distribution shift", not "calibrated".
Few-shot alternative: "50-shot is almost as calibrated as the finetuned setup" (k random training examples placed in the prompt). Note the training label is sub-task accuracy, not per-item correctness; the authors say superficial heuristics such as integer size "cannot explain the performance of verbalized probability".
Caveat: CalibratedMath is arithmetic; old model.
SOURCE: Lin, Hilton, Evans 2022, "Teaching models to express their uncertainty in words", https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14334 (fetched PDF, Sec. 3, Table 1, Fig. 6).
COSTS: fine-tuning (original setup: OpenAI API on GPT-3 davinci) or a 50-shot prompt; labelled sub-task accuracy table; text only at inference; no logprobs for the verbalized arm.

FINE-TUNING ON A THOUSAND GRADED EXAMPLES BEATS PROMPTING FOR OPEN MODELS (KAPOOR ET AL. 2024)
Claim: "prompting on its own is insufficient to achieve good calibration" and "a thousand graded examples are sufficient to outperform baseline methods"; "ECE doesn't appear to improve with the abilities of the underlying model, and while AUROC does show small improvements ... the gap between zero-shot methods and fine-tuning for uncertainties remains large." Prompting methods compared: zero-shot classifier P(True), verbalized, sampling-cluster. "around 1000 labeled examples is enough to improve performance over simpler baselines ... the marginal benefit of additional data points decreases after around 5000 examples." A KL regularizer matters (Table 1: ECE 29.9% without, 10.8% with, AUROC 70.2 vs 71.6, mean of six base models).
Models: LLaMA-2 7B/13B, Mistral 7B and chat variants, LoRA. Tension with Tian 2023: Tian finds verbalized confidence helpful for strong closed RLHF models; Kapoor finds prompting poor for the open models it tested. Both can be true; the model family matters.
Cost they report: "Each training run takes approximately 1-3 GPU days with 4 NVIDIA RTX8000 (48GB) GPUs." The sampling baseline needs K=10 samples plus a semantic-similarity model.
For this requester: training is out of reach; the transferable idea is the graded set. A few hundred to a thousand graded items is what you need anyway to measure and to recalibrate (next section).
SOURCE: Kapoor, Gruver, Roberts, et al. 2024, NeurIPS, "Large Language Models Must Be Taught to Know What They Don't Know", https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08391 (fetched PDF, abstract, Sec. 3-4, Table 1).
COSTS: GPU (1 to 3 days on 4 x 48GB), open weights, ~1000 graded items.

TEXT-ONLY RECALIBRATION: MAP STATED CONFIDENCE TO MEASURED ACCURACY ON A HELD-OUT SET
Tian et al. do this without any training: their "Ling. 1S-opt." method has the model use words ("Almost certain, Likely, ..., Almost no chance") and then replaces each word's assumed probability with measured accuracy from a held-out calibration set: "Ling. 1S-opt. uses a held out set of calibration questions and answers to compute the average accuracy for each likelihood expression." The unoptimized mapping from a social-media survey is the comparison. gpt-3.5-turbo ECE, human-survey map vs optimised map: SciQ 0.166 vs 0.064; TruthfulQA 0.306 vs 0.125; TriviaQA 0.062 vs 0.058. gpt-4: SciQ 0.148 vs 0.028; TruthfulQA 0.241 vs 0.082. Phrases the model rarely uses fall back to the survey probability (per the paper).
Mechanic: bucket the agent's stated confidences, compute the observed hit rate per bucket on graded held-out items, use that table as the real probability. Caveats from the same tables: for gpt-4 AUC barely moves (0.931 vs 0.927 TriviaQA), so recalibration repairs calibration not ranking; and (my inference, not tested here) a map fit on one task mix may not hold when the mix shifts. Guo et al. temperature scaling does the same job for logits ("a single scalar parameter T") but needs logits.
SOURCE: Tian et al. 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14975 (Sec. 2 and Tables 1-2); Guo et al. 2017, https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599 (Sec. 4.2).
COSTS: held-out graded set (size not specified in the fetched text; a few hundred is my assumption); text only; no logprobs for the word-map method.

SELECTIVE PREDICTION: PICKING THE ABSTAIN THRESHOLD FROM DATA
Method: sort held-out items by confidence, choose the cutoff that gives a target error among answered items, abstain below it. Geifman and El-Yaniv's SGR algorithm does it with a statistical guarantee: it "performs a binary search to find the optimal bound guaranteeing the required risk with sufficient confidence." Result on ImageNet top-5 (Table 6): desired risk 0.02, test risk 0.0189, test coverage 0.5935, at delta = 0.001; abstract: "2% error ... can be guaranteed with probability 99.9%, and almost 60% test coverage." The guarantee is stated as Pr{R(f,g) > r_target} < delta, "where the probability is over training samples, Sm, sampled i.i.d. from the unknown underlying distribution", so it assumes the labelled sample matches the test distribution.
When the data shifts, the raw confidence score fails: Kamath et al.: "Abstention policies based solely on the model's softmax probabilities fare poorly, since models are overconfident on out-of-domain inputs." Numbers, mixed in-domain and out-of-domain QA: coverage at 80% accuracy: MaxProb 48.23%, trained calibrator 56.06%, optimal 74.92%; at 90% accuracy: 21.07% vs 29.42% vs 66.59%. A calibrator trained to predict "was the model right" using some out-of-domain examples gained about 8 points but left a 19-point gap to optimal.
For the requester (inference): a threshold set on today's task mix may over- or under-abstain on tomorrow's. Re-fit when the task mix changes.
SOURCE: Geifman, El-Yaniv 2017, https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500 (Sec. 2-3, Table 6); Kamath, Jia, Liang 2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09462 (abstract, Table 1) (fetched PDFs).
COSTS: labelled held-out set with the agent's confidence per item; a sort and a threshold; no GPU; any scalar score works.

ABSTAIN VIA PROMPT VS TRAINING: CHENG ET AL. 2024 (IDK DATASET)
Setting: Llama-2-chat assistants; an "Idk" dataset built per model from its own answers, with an "Ik threshold" deciding what counts as known. Metrics: Ik-Ik (answered correctly), Ik-Idk (correctly refused), TRUTHFUL = sum. Table 1, TriviaQA test (45.05% known, 54.95% unknown): prompting to refuse: 37.36 / 29.58 / TRUTHFUL 66.93; SFT 28.57 / 46.19 / 74.75; DPO 39.30 / 38.59 / 77.89; best-of-N 38.37 / 40.59 / 78.96. Natural Questions: prompting 61.47, SFT 69.92, DPO 66.51.
Failure modes named in the paper: prompting leaves many unknowns answered (Ik-Idk 29.58 of a possible 54.95). SFT overshoots: "it also tends to make the model more convervative [sic], leading to incorrect refusals to answer some questions that it actually knows" (Ik-Ik fell 37.36 to 28.57). "Using preference-aware optimization, like DPO, can alleviate the model's excessive conservatism." Also: "the higher the Ik threshold, the greater the total number of Ik-Ik and Ik-Idk questions" so the labelling threshold sets the refusal rate.
Takeaway: abstention that gets used appropriately means tracking two rates, over-refusal and under-refusal, since each training/prompt method moved one at the expense of the other. A single "refusal rate" hides this.
SOURCE: Cheng, Sun, Liu, Zhang, Yin, Li, Li, He, Chen, Qiu 2024, "Can AI Assistants Know What They Don't Know?", https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13275 (fetched PDF, Table 1, Fig. 2).
COSTS: prompting arm: text only. SFT/DPO/BoN arms: GPU fine-tuning plus a reward model, and the per-model sampled-answer dataset.

ABSTENTION BENCHMARK: SCALE AND REASONING TRAINING DO NOT FIX IT (ABSTENTIONBENCH 2025)
20 datasets, 20 frontier LLMs. Quotes: "abstention is an unsolved problem, and one where scaling models is of little use"; "reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention (by 24% on average), even for math and science domains"; "while a carefully crafted system prompt can boost abstention in practice, it does not resolve models' fundamental inability to reason about uncertainty"; "scaling reasoning token budget substantially increases accuracy on reasoning tasks, it generally further worsens abstention." Spread across data types: "ranging from near-perfect performance on BIG-Bench Known Unknowns, down to near-zero recall on MediQ."
Metrics: abstention recall (proportion abstained when abstention was correct), plus precision and F1 "to account for over-abstention"; they focus on recall because "models generally exhibit high abstention precision" (i.e. under-use is the dominant failure here).
Measurement caveat: abstention is detected by a Llama 3.1 8B judge that "obtained 88% accuracy on a manually annotated sample"; 12% of labels may be wrong.
System prompt: they reproduce it; it says "If a question is unanswerable, because there is insufficient evidence ..., don't provide a guess and respond 'I don't know'", plus "you ask for clarification". Effect reported in a figure without numbers in text; precision did not degrade significantly.
SOURCE: Kirichenko, Ibrahim, Chaudhuri, Bell 2025, "AbstentionBench", https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09038 (fetched PDF, abstract, Sec. 3.4, 4.1-4.4, App. D.5).
COSTS: text only; a judge model; labelled answerable/unanswerable items.

R-TUNING AND SELFAWARE: CLAIM VS TABLE
R-Tuning (refusal-aware instruction tuning, LLaMA 3B/7B/13B, A100-40GB) trains the model to refuse questions outside what the pretrained model already knows, and scores confidence ranking with Average Precision (AP). Text claim: "it is observed from Table 1 that R-Tuning outperforms Vanilla in terms of the AP score." Table 1 has 12 cells; R-Tuning is lower in two: LLaMA-7B ParaRel out-of-domain 74.61 vs 78.08 and OpenLLaMA-3B MMLU out-of-domain 24.75 vs 26.08. In-domain ParaRel gaps are small (93.64 vs 93.32; 94.44 vs 94.00); big wins are at 13B (MMLU in-domain 68.87 vs 51.93). Read "consistently better" as "better in 10 of 12 cells".
SelfAware (Yin et al. 2023): 1,032 unanswerable and 2,337 answerable questions; refusal is detected by text similarity to reference "uncertain" sentences (SimCSE, threshold 0.75), scored with F1. Result: "GPT-4, measures at 75.47%, ... human self-knowledge, which is rated at 84.93%"; "in-context learning and instruction tuning can effectively enhance the self-knowledge." The detector's own agreement with humans at 0.75: precision 100, recall 85, F1 91.89 (their Table 2), so some refusals are missed.
SOURCE: Zhang et al. 2023, "R-Tuning", https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09677 (Table 1, Sec. 3.3, 4.1); Yin et al. 2023, "Do Large Language Models Know What They Don't Know?", https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18153 (fetched PDFs).
COSTS: R-Tuning: GPU fine-tuning, a graded training set for the "known" split. SelfAware: text only, a similarity model.

TRAINING A P(IK) PREDICTOR (KADAVATH ET AL. 2022)
P(IK) = the probability that "I know" the answer before seeing any proposed answer. Training: add a value head and fine-tune the whole model; labels come from sampling: "we sample 30 answers (at temperature = 1) from each TriviaQA question ... ground truth P(IK) scores." Result on the training distribution: "P(IK) is very well calibrated on TriviaQA." Out of distribution (trained on TriviaQA, tested on Lambada, arithmetic, GSM8k, code): the abstract says models "partially generalize across tasks, though they struggle with calibration of P(IK) on new tasks", and the body: "P(IK) tends to be poorly calibrated on these other distributions." P(IK) rose with source materials and correct hints in context. "Because we're adding a new untrained head, language models do not perform well zero or few-shot at predicting P(IK), so we need to finetune them."
Warning from the authors on the label: "Name a composite integer in the range [1,100]" can give an apparent P(IK) = 0.75 by chance, so P(IK) can be inflated by questions that are easy to guess.
Numeric AUROC/ECE for P(IK) are in figures only; I did not read values. See NOT FOUND list.
SOURCE: Kadavath et al. 2022, https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05221 (abstract, Sec. 5.1, 5.2, footnote 5) (fetched PDF).
COSTS: GPU fine-tuning of the full model (52B in the paper); logprob-free but needs weights; 30 samples per training question.

LLM-AS-JUDGE VERBALIZED CONFIDENCE ON POST-2025 MODELS (HSIAO 2026)
Claim reversing 2023 advice: on frontier proprietary models, "the standard advice to prefer log-probabilities no longer holds on post-2025 models, where verbalized confidence is the better signal." Logprobs are not available anyway: "Claude never did, and Gemini dropped them at 3.1 Pro."
Recipe: add an overconfidence advisory and a self-debate to a rubric prompt. Advisory wording quoted: "You have been consistently overconfident in past evaluations..." Self-debate: argue True, argue False, then explain and answer. Ablation: "the advisory is the primary calibration lever ... self-debate on its own barely moves AECE." Table 2 (AggreFact, adaptive-bin ECE, rubric vs recipe): gpt-4o 8.0 to 4.1; gpt-4.1 11.2 to 4.4; sonnet-4.5 9.1 to 3.3; sonnet-4.6 10.2 to 8.1; gpt-5.4 12.7 to 9.1; gemini-3.1-pro 13.1 to 11.2. Balanced accuracy change within about plus or minus 3 points; older models lose accuracy, newer do not.
Conflict with Xiong 2023 (persona prompts "confident"/"cautious" had no effect): this advisory is a different instruction (telling the model it has been overconfident) and a different model generation. Treat as unreplicated.
Weight: single author, one preprint (arXiv v1, 10 Sep 2026), binary faithfulness plus SummEval/HelpSteer2; post-recipe AECE is still about 3 to 13 points. Verify on your own graded set.
SOURCE: Hsiao 2026, "Rethinking Verbalized Confidence for LLM-as-a-Judge", https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10996 (fetched PDF, abstract, Method, Tables 2-3).
COSTS: text only; one call per item (self-debate is inside the same call); graded set.

REASONING MODELS: CONFIDENCE CONCENTRATES ON HIGH VALUES (ABSTRACT-ONLY, UNVERIFIED BODY)
Only the arXiv abstract was fetched (export.arxiv.org API). Wang et al. 2026, arXiv 2609.32470: "Large reasoning models (LRMs) often suffer from overconfidence when expressing their uncertainty ... off-the-shelf LRMs exhibit a confidence prior heavily concentrated on a few high values, which persists throughout RL." They fix it with an extra SFT stage to spread confidence targets before RL, then report lower calibration error over "16 mathematical and general reasoning benchmarks." No numbers were read. Jang et al. 2025, arXiv 2506.03723 (abstract only): "supervised fine-tuning with scalar confidence labels alone suffices to elicit self-verification behavior"; models write longer, self-checking responses for low-confidence queries.
Use: consistent with Xiong's 80-100 clustering on older models; confidence collapse onto a few high values has been reported on reasoning models. Not a measurement I can quote numerically.
SOURCE: abstracts at https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32470 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03723 (fetched via https://export.arxiv.org/api/query?id_list=...).
COSTS: reading the full papers; the training methods need GPU RL/SFT.
