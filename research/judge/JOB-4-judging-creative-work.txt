JOB 4 - Automated judgement of work with no single correct output (video edits, images, design, prose)
Topic: how creative/aesthetic output is scored automatically, what correlates with humans, film-specific metrics, rule-breaking, failure modes
Date: 2026-10-03
Sections: 20 (sections 16 and 17 are NOT FOUND sections; the rest are sourced findings)
NOT FOUND sub-questions: 4 (automated continuity/jump-cut/180-rule metrics; automated "deliberate rule-break that works"; any scorer validated on human-made edits; VBench's self-stated limits on its aesthetic metric)
All papers below were fetched this session (arXiv PDFs read as text, repo/blog pages fetched) and quoted from the fetched text. Section 14 contains one measurement I ran myself, labelled as such.

1. LAION aesthetic predictor: what it is, and how it was chosen
It is a linear layer on frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 embeddings. The authors trained several heads and picked by eye, not by held-out human agreement: "we found that a simple linear model on the top of CLIP ViT/14 produced in our subjective view the visually most appealing results when used to rank images of LAION-5B. (Even though other MLPs with e.g. Relu functions produced slightly lower MSE and MAE loss values.)" Training data: SAC (AI-image ratings), LAION-Logos, and AVA (photos from dpchallenge.com rated 1-10). The README describes it as "A linear estimator on top of clip to predict the aesthetic quality of pictures". VBench uses this exact predictor for its Aesthetic Quality dimension, frame by frame. Its measured agreement with human pairwise preference is in section 3 (56.8 percent, same as the random baseline) and section 4 (varies 57.4 to 76.8 by test set).
SOURCE: LAION blog "LAION-Aesthetics" https://laion.ai/blog/laion-aesthetics/ ; repo https://raw.githubusercontent.com/LAION-AI/aesthetic-predictor/main/README.md ; VBench arXiv 2311.17982 https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17982 ("We evaluate the artistic and beauty value perceived by humans towards each video frame using the LAION aesthetic predictor")
COSTS: CPU is enough for a few images (CLIP ViT-L/14 forward pass plus one linear layer); a small GPU for video frames. No training.

2. NIMA (Talebi and Milanfar, 2017/2018): predicts the distribution of human ratings
NIMA predicts the distribution of opinion scores rather than a single mean: "Despite the subjective nature of this problem, most existing methods only predict the mean opinion score ... Our approach differs from others in that we predict the distribution of human opinion scores". Reported on AVA (about 255,000 photos): NIMA(Inception-v2) accuracy 81.51 percent on the two-class split at score 5, mean-score LCC 0.636 and SRCC 0.612. Cross-dataset: "training on AVA and testing on LIVE results in 0.552 and 0.543 linear and rank correlations ... However, training on LIVE and testing on AVA leads to 0.238 and 0.2". So a rank correlation near 0.6 is the in-distribution ceiling for photo aesthetics, and it degrades sharply out of distribution. The predicted spread (std of ratings) is the useful part for a judge: a high-variance image is one where humans disagree.
SOURCE: Talebi and Milanfar, "NIMA: Neural Image Assessment", 2017 (IEEE TIP 2018) https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.05424
COSTS: Original used ImageNet backbones (MobileNet/VGG16/Inception-v2); inference is one forward pass, runs on CPU. Third-party reimplementations exist but I did not fetch any; weights not verified.

3. PickScore / Pick-a-Pic (Kirstain et al., 2023): the cleanest measurement of "does the scorer beat chance"
CLIP-H fine-tuned on real users' pairwise choices. Pairwise accuracy on the test set: "a 70.5% accuracy rate, compared to humans' 68.0%, while zero-shot CLIP-H (60.8%) and the popular aesthetics predictor [14] (56.8%) perform closer to chance (56.8%)". Their random baseline is 56.8 because ties are allowed. Model-level ranking: Elo from 14,000 real-user preferences over 45 model configurations, correlation with real users "PickScore ... (0.790 ± 0.054) ... CLIP-H (0.313 ± 0.075), ImageReward (0.492 ± 0.086), and HPS (0.670 ± 0.071)". On 100 MS-COCO captions with human rankings, PickScore 0.917 and FID -0.900 (negative: FID prefers images humans like less; authors blame high guidance scale). Caveats: the 68.0 "human" figure is friends and colleagues of the authors, blind to the original user's intent; absolute accuracy of a single judge on a single pair is modest even for the best scorer.
SOURCE: Kirstain, Polyak, Singer, Matiana et al., "Pick-a-Pic: An Open Dataset of User Preferences for Text-to-Image Generation", 2023 https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01569
COSTS: CLIP-H inference (about 1B parameters); a laptop GPU or patient CPU. Prompt text is required as input, so it scores prompt-image pairs, not freestanding images.

4. HPS v2 and ImageReward: human ceiling, and test-set dependence
HPS v2 Table 6 (preference prediction accuracy, two test sets): Aesthetic Score Predictor 57.4 on the ImageReward test set and 76.8 on the HPD v2 test set; PickScore 62.9 and 79.8; ImageReward 65.1 and 74.0; HPS v2 65.7 and 83.3; "Single Human vs. Single Human" 65.3 and 78.1; "Single Human vs. Averaged Human" 53.9 and 85.0. Same scorer, 19 points apart by test set, so any single accuracy number is conditional on the image distribution. ImageReward's own agreement table (Table 2a) reports researcher-vs-researcher 71.2 percent, researcher-vs-annotator 65.3 percent, researcher-vs-annotator-ensemble 73.4 percent, and its scorer at 65.14 percent preference accuracy; "human preference for image fidelity is far more complex than aesthetics". Lesson: a scorer at the single-human-vs-single-human level is at the noise ceiling, and you cannot tell whether it is right on any one pair.
SOURCE: Wu et al., "Human Preference Score v2", 2023 https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09341 (Table 6); Xu et al., "ImageReward", 2023 https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05977 (Table 2a, Table 3)
COSTS: CLIP-sized models; HPS v2 and ImageReward have public code per the abstracts. Laptop GPU or CPU.

5. VBench (Huang et al., 2023): what it checks and what its human-alignment number does and does not show
16 dimensions, each with its own detector: aesthetic = LAION predictor; imaging quality; subject/background consistency (DINO/CLIP features); temporal flickering; motion smoothness; dynamic degree (RAFT optical flow). Human alignment (Figure 5, Table A5) is Spearman rho per dimension between VBench win ratio and human win ratio, from 0.9651 for subject consistency down to a lowest shown value of 0.8037. Two limits visible in the paper itself: (a) the correlation is computed over a handful of models ("the win ratio on evaluation results predicted by VBench and Human across four models and all dimensions"), so rho is over four points per dimension, which is model-level ranking, not per-video accuracy; (b) the consistency metrics are trivially maximised by doing nothing: "a completely static video corresponds to the 100% score." VideoScore (section 7) states the practical consequence: "most models have subject/background consistency scores over 97% in VBench, which is a massive overestimation of the current T2V models' true capability." VBench's own Limitations section (Appendix L) lists only model coverage and other tasks; it does not discuss metric validity. VBench-2.0 (2025) says earlier dimensions "mainly represent superficial faithfulness, which focus on whether the video appears visually convincing".
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17982 (Sec 4.2, Appendix J.2, L, Table A5); https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21755 (abstract)
COSTS: Code is open; several models (DINO, RAFT, CLIP, LAION aesthetic) each run per video; a GPU is advisable, a laptop GPU is workable for a few clips. Built for text-to-video outputs, not edits.

6. EvalCrafter (Liu et al., 2023): fit metric weights to human scores, and a warning about CLIPScore
17 objective metrics grouped as visual quality, content quality, motion quality, text-video alignment; coefficients are fitted to human opinion: "our final score shows a higher correlation than simply averaging the metrics". Their ablation: "Metrics like Warp Error, CLIP-Temp, VQA_T, and VQA_A seem to perform well ... some metrics are not as good as we think. The ClipScore especially, which is a widely used metric in previous works, only has Spearsman's rho 6.3 and Kendall's phi 4.3". Some metrics have negative correlation with humans (Color-Score, OCR-Score on alignment). Stated limitation: "The labels used for alignment are collected from only fewer human annotators, which may introduce some bias". Only 700 prompts. The fitted weights are fit and checked on the same pool (20 percent held out), so this is the cheap recipe (weighted sum of off-the-shelf metrics calibrated to your own human labels) but it needs your own labels.
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11440 (Sec 5.1, Finding 9, Sec 5.3)
COSTS: Many off-the-shelf models; GPU helpful. Calibration step needs human scores of your own, a few hundred rated items minimum (my judgement, not from the paper).

7. VideoScore (He et al., EMNLP 2024): trained video scorer; the best measured per-video agreement, and the baselines it beats
Trained on VideoFeedback (37.6K videos, five human-rated aspects) from a Mantis-Idefics2-8B backbone. Spearman with humans on its own test set: 77.1 average; GPT-4o 23.0; Gemini-1.5-Pro 16.9; Gemini-1.5-Flash 20.8; feature metrics mostly negative: "PIQE, BRISQUE, CLIPScore, and X-CLIP-Score get nearly all negative correlations for all 5 aspects. This proves the image quality assessment metrics cannot be easily adapted to the video quality assessment task." Held-out: 59.5 Spearman on EvalCrafter text-video alignment; 78.5 pairwise accuracy on GenAI-Bench video preference; 72.1 average on five VBench aspects. Caveats: all training and test videos are machine-generated; the 77.1 is in-distribution; no human-edited footage was tested. Training used "8 A100 (80G) GPUs, finishing in 6 hours" (you cannot repeat this); inference of an 8B model needs a quantised model on a small GPU (my estimate, not tested).
SOURCE: He, Jiang, Zhang, Ku et al., "VideoScore", 2024 https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15252 (Tables 4, 5; Sec 4.4)
COSTS: Use released weights for inference only; 8B VLM, about 16 GB in fp16, less if quantised. Training is out of reach.

8. GenAI-Arena (Jiang et al., 2024): human votes as ground truth, and multimodal LLMs as judges of them
Chatbot-Arena style: anonymous pairs, four vote options ("left is better, right is better, tie, both are bad"), Bradley-Terry Elo with bootstrapped confidence intervals. Asked to mimic these votes (exact match on 4 labels, random = 25.16 on video), GPT-4o scores 48.46 on video generation and 49.19 on average, Gemini-1.5-Pro 46.21 video; open-source MLLMs mostly 0-30. Paper: "existing multimodal models are still lagging in assessing the generated visual content". Quality of the human votes: expert review of 350 sampled votes found 76.24 percent "clearly reasonable", 16.83 percent "vague", 6.93 percent "wrong". Elo weakness they report: it "can be biased by the imbalance of 'easy games' and 'hard games'". Take-away: an LLM judge sees roughly half of human votes correctly where chance is a quarter, and human votes themselves are about three-quarters clean.
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04485 (Table 7, Table 6, Sec 3)
COSTS: Reading results is free; running your own arena needs voters. Judging with a hosted multimodal LLM needs an API; the paper's open-source MLLMs are runnable locally but score far worse.

9. LLM-as-judge mechanics (MT-Bench, Zheng et al., 2023): what to copy
Strong-LLM pairwise judges reach "over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans" on chat answers; this is text, not video. Documented biases: "position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias". Mitigation that costs nothing: "call a judge twice by swapping the order of two answers and only declare a win when an answer is preferred in both orders. If the results are inconsistent after swapping, we can call it a tie." Verbosity test: judges failed a "repetitive list" attack (padding the same content twice) where only GPT-4 resisted well. The swap-and-require-both-orders rule and a padding/identical-answer control are directly reusable for judging two cuts of the same footage.
SOURCE: Zheng, Chiang, Sheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", 2023 https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685 (Sec 3.3, 4.2)
COSTS: Any capable LLM; 2x calls per comparison. For video it needs frames or a VLM; text-only judges cannot see a cut.

10. LLM-as-judge for creative work: measured to be weak
Lu et al. 2025 compare four creativity measures against human-labelled creative vs uncreative sets in creative writing, problem-solving, and research ideation. LLM judge best configuration was Claude 3.7 Sonnet. Findings in their words: "metrics that distinguish creativity in one domain fail in others"; LLM-as-a-Judge shows "inconsistent judgments under minor prompt variations" (flipping "conventional?" to "unconventional?" changed its overall preference); and its predictions are "only marginally better than random guessing". Correlation with human reviewers on research-ideation contribution: Pearson 0.159, best 0.234 (GPT-4o). This is prose and ideas, not video; it is the closest fetched evidence for LLM judgement of open-ended creative output, and it is negative.
SOURCE: Lu, Liu, Lu, Tian, "Rethinking Creativity Evaluation: A Critical Analysis of Existing Creativity Evaluations", 2025 https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05470
COSTS: LLM API or local LLM; their datasets small.

11. DOVER (Wu et al., 2022): cheap pretrained video quality scorer, technical vs aesthetic
Trained on user-generated video; two branches, aesthetic (downsampled view) and technical (raw-patch view). Their human study (DIVIDE-3k, 3,590 videos): overall opinion correlates with a mix of both, "the weighted sum of both perspectives is a better approximation of overall quality than either single perspective"; Spearman between the aesthetic and technical human scores is 0.8752, so the two are not independent. Benchmark SROCC: LSVQ test 0.888, KoNViD-1k 0.884, LIVE-VQC 0.832; cost reported "CPU Time 9.7s, GPU Time 47ms" for a 1080P 10-second video (the fastest-class entries). It scores frame quality (blur, noise, exposure, composition-like content appeal). It says nothing about cut choice or story.
SOURCE: Wu, Zhang, Liao, Chen et al., "Exploring Video Quality Assessment on User Generated Contents from Aesthetic and Technical Perspectives", 2022 https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04894 (Tables 1, 3, Sec 3.3)
COSTS: Pretrained weights; runs on CPU at about 10 s per 10 s of 1080p footage as reported; small GPU makes it near real-time.

12. Shot-length statistics (Cinemetrics, ASL, Cutting et al.): measured, but not shown to predict quality
Cinemetrics is a measurement tool and database ("in film studies, we time shots", Cinemetrics home page). Average Shot Length (ASL) is "by far the most common physical metric for film" (Cutting, Brunick, DeLong 2011). Redfern 2022 argues ASL is the wrong summary: "it is not a measure of cutting rate" and recommends analysing rates and distributions. Cutting, DeLong and Nothelfer (2010) analysed 150 Hollywood films 1935-2005: shot lengths became "increasingly more correlated in length with their neighbors" and power spectra approached 1/f; the autoregressive index correlated with release year r = .44. The key negative result: "one might assume that viewers like better those films with a shot structure closer to a 1/f pattern. However, this is not the case ... their [IMDb] ratings do not correlate with film slopes (r = -.089, n.s.)." Cutting 2011: shots are longer at quarter boundaries and shorter mid-quarter. Conclusion: shot-length structure is a descriptive fingerprint of how a film is paced; no source I fetched shows it predicts whether an edit is good.
SOURCE: https://cinemetrics.uchicago.edu/ ; Cutting, DeLong, Nothelfer, Psychological Science 21(3), 2010 https://jordandelong.com/pubs/2010/AttentionEvolution.pdf ; Cutting, Brunick, DeLong, Projections 5(1), 2011 https://filmoterapia.pl/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/How-Act-Structure-Sculpts-Shot-Lengths-and-Shot-Transitions-in-Hollywood-Film.pdf ; Redfern, Wide Screen 9(1), 2022 https://widescreenjournal.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/formatted-cutting-rates.pdf
COSTS: Free; the numbers come from a list of cut timestamps. Computing ASL, median, spread and neighbour autocorrelation is a few lines of numpy on a CPU.

13. Shot boundary detection: TransNet V2 and PySceneDetect (the cut list that every pacing metric depends on)
TransNet V2 reports F1 on ClipShots 77.9, BBC 96.2, RAI 93.9; its authors: "one hundred percent accuracy is still an unreachable ideal". Cutting 2011 notes viewers "often miss a large number of [cuts] ... and computer techniques are scarcely any better" (2006 benchmark). PySceneDetect (BSD, v0.7.1) offers ContentDetector, AdaptiveDetector (handles fast camera movement better), ThresholdDetector (fades) and ships a benchmark harness with TRECVID-style scoring on BBC Planet Earth, AutoShot and ClipShots.
SOURCE: Soucek and Lokoc, "TransNet V2", 2020 https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.04838 (Table 2); https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Breakthrough/PySceneDetect/main/README.md ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Breakthrough/PySceneDetect/main/benchmark/README.md
COSTS: PySceneDetect: CPU only, pip install scenedetect opencv-python. TransNet V2: pretrained weights, a laptop GPU helps, it also runs on CPU for short clips (not tested by me).

14. My own test: PySceneDetect's default silently drops short shots, which biases ASL upward (measurement run this session, synthetic)
I generated a 24 fps video of 10 synthetic shots (known lengths 2.0, 0.5, 4.0, 1.0, 1.5, 0.5, 0.5, 3.0, 1.25, 2.5 s) and ran scenedetect 0.7.1 detect(video, ContentDetector()) on CPU: 0.19 s, found 7 shots [2.0, 4.5, 1.0, 1.5, 4.0, 1.25, 2.5]; it merged the 0.5 s shots into their neighbours, reporting ASL 2.39 s against a true 1.88 s. With ContentDetector(min_scene_len=1) it recovered all 10 lengths exactly. Source of the cause: the code has "min_scene_len: TimecodeLike = 15" (frames) and "Once a cut is detected, this much time must pass before a new one can". For fast-cut montage this is exactly the region a pacing judge cares about. This is a toy (clean synthetic hard cuts); real footage with dissolves and flashes will be harder. If you use shot detection as a judge, set min_scene_len to 1 frame and check against a hand-marked cut list for one clip.
SOURCE: my script run in this session; detector parameter https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Breakthrough/PySceneDetect/main/scenedetect/detectors/content_detector.py
COSTS: CPU, under a second per clip; pip install only.

15. Cut-type recognition and cut plausibility: the only learned "does this cut work" models I found
MovieCuts (Pardo et al., 2021): 173,967 clips labelled with ten cut types by professional editors' taxonomy, including Cutting on Action ("Cutting from one shot to another while the subject is still in motion"), Match Cut, Smash Cut, J Cut, L Cut. Best model reaches only "47.7% mAP, which suggests that the task is challenging and that attaining highly accurate Cut type recognition is an open research problem". Label quality: annotations had 90.5 percent precision and 88.2 percent recall against five editors' consensus on 2,000 cuts. Learning to Cut by Watching Movies (same authors) ranks how plausible a cut is, trained by contrasting real cuts from edited films with artificial ones. Human study, ten professional editors, pairwise versus professional cuts: random cuts were preferred over the professional cut 26.7 percent of the time, an audio-visual baseline 27.5 percent, their model 32.5 percent (so the pros win 73.3 / 72.5 / 67.5). Authors: "there is still a long way to generate cuts with professional quality." They also chose editors over general audience because "the general population is unable to perceive the fine-grained details of good cuts."
SOURCE: Pardo, Heilbron, Alcazar, Thabet, "MovieCuts" 2021 https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05569 ; "Learning to Cut by Watching Movies" 2021 https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04294 (Sec 4.3, Fig 5)
COSTS: Code and data are stated public in the abstract; inference on a laptop GPU. The study design (judge vs random cuts vs pro cuts, ten editors) is the reusable part; it costs paid expert time.

16. NOT FOUND: automated metrics for continuity, jump cuts, 180-degree rule, eyeline match
I searched arXiv (export API) for film continuity editing, 180-degree rule, jump cut detection, film grammar violations, match cut detection and found no paper that defines or validates an automated checker. The only adjacent item is MovieCuts' classifier for cut types (section 15), which labels what a cut is, not whether it is a continuity error. I also found no industry tooling documentation I could fetch (Premiere, Resolve, etc. not tested). Treat any "continuity score" as something you would have to build and validate yourself.
SOURCE: arXiv API queries run this session (none relevant returned); closest: https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05569
COSTS: n/a

17. NOT FOUND: automated separation of "breaks a rule" from "breaks it deliberately and it works"
No fetched paper offers a method. Closest evidence, all indirect. (1) MovieCuts notes Smash and Match cuts "emerge scarcely in the dataset, it is still important to recognize these types of cuts, which can be considered the most creative ones": rule-bending cuts are rare, so a model trained on frequency learns them poorly. (2) Learning to Cut trains to "discriminate between real and artificial cuts", i.e. it learns convention; by construction it will score unconventional-but-effective cuts low (my inference, not tested). (3) Lu et al. tried the nearest thing, requiring a high score on both novelty and acceptability from an LLM judge, and got accuracy "0.51 and 0.54 respectively - both close to random guessing" against human labels. (4) Cutting 2010 found even a clear statistical regularity (1/f shot structure) uncorrelated with audience ratings. Practical reading: today the separation is done by humans, with a stated intent (a note saying why the rule is broken) that a judge can check against the cut, not by a scorer detecting intent.
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05569 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04294 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.05470 ; https://jordandelong.com/pubs/2010/AttentionEvolution.pdf
COSTS: n/a for the NOT FOUND; the stated-intent check costs one LLM call per flagged cut plus a human spot check.

18. Failure mode: optimising against an aesthetic scorer produces images that score high and look wrong
Hong et al. 2026 train text-to-image models with RL against reward models and report: "the model trained with HPS shows rising HPS reward and aesthetic scores but experiences clear drops on compositional correctness and semantic consistency"; "over-emphasizing colorfulness often leads to unrealistic and over-saturated images"; other metrics (PickScore, VQAScore, UnifiedReward) "show no consistent upward trend across training steps, regardless of reward types"; a shared failure across reward models is "the generation of artifact-prone images"; ensembling rewards "can only partially mitigate this issue". Related: Pick-a-Pic found higher guidance scales (more vivid images) won human votes but worsened FID, and CLIP-H plus the LAION aesthetic head sit near chance on pairwise preference (section 3). Meaning for an agent that edits: if the agent can see the judge's score and iterate, expect it to find whatever the scorer overvalues (saturation, contrast, sharpness). Keep the scorer hidden from the producer, or rotate and hold out a second scorer.
SOURCE: Hong, Kao, Zhou, Hsieh, "Understanding Reward Hacking in Text-to-Image Reinforcement Learning", 2026 https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03468 (Sec 3, Fig 1)
COSTS: The finding is free to apply; replicating the study needs RL training on GPUs.

19. How to make a judge that can fail: anchors, controls and paired checks that are actually in the literature
Four mechanisms, each from a fetched source. (a) Empirical floor and ceiling: VBench built "Empirical Min" from "videos of i.i.d. Gaussian noise" and from "randomly sample[d] frames from different WebVid-10M videos", and Empirical Max from real videos, so each metric has a known bad and good anchor; a scorer that cannot separate noise or shuffled frames from real footage is broken. (b) Random-cut control: Learning to Cut's human study includes randomly placed cuts as an option and shows editors pick the professional cut 73.3 percent of the time against random; a judge on your edits should be run on your cut versus a randomised-cut version of the same footage and must prefer yours well above 50 percent. (c) Order swap: MT-Bench: only declare a win if the same answer wins in both orders, else tie. (d) Human ceiling: report agreement with a single human against human-human agreement (HPS v2: 65.3 and 78.1 on its two test sets, section 4); a judge cannot be trusted above that ceiling on single pairs. Also record per-aspect scores rather than one number (VBench, VideoScore, DOVER all decompose).
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17982 (Appendix J.2); https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04294 (Sec 4.3); https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685 (Sec 3.3); https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09341 (Table 6)
COSTS: All controls are cheap to run: one extra render per control, extra judge calls. Human ceiling needs at least two people rating the same few dozen pairs.

Verdict
For images there are pretrained scorers with measured agreement against human pairwise votes, but the best ones agree with a single human about as well as humans agree with each other (about 65-78 percent pairwise). For AI-generated video there are benchmarks and one trained scorer (VideoScore). For human-made video EDITS (cut choice, pacing, continuity) there is no validated automated quality scorer in anything I could fetch. What exists is measurement of properties (shot lengths, cut type, quality of frames) plus one research model that ranks cut plausibility. Whether a property matters for quality is mostly untested, and one test that was run came back null (section 12).
SOURCE: Pick-a-Pic / PickScore, Kirstain et al., 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01569 ; Human Preference Score v2, Wu et al., 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09341 ; ImageReward, Xu et al., 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05977 ; VBench, Huang et al., 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17982 ; VideoScore, He et al., 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15252 ; Cutting, DeLong, Nothelfer, Psychological Science 2010, https://jordandelong.com/pubs/2010/AttentionEvolution.pdf ; Learning to Cut by Watching Movies, Pardo et al., 2021, https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04294
COSTS: n/a
