SECTION 1. Evaluator LLMs miss over half of planted defects (negative result, lead finding)
Doddapaneni et al. built 2400 answers, each with one deliberate defect from 22 categories. A working judge must score the defective answer lower than the gold answer. Abstract: "Our findings reveal significant shortcomings in current Evaluator LLMs, which failed to identify quality drops in over 50% of cases on average." Even trivial defects slipped through: "even basic perturbation categories, such as, fluency perturbations (e.g. spellings and grammar) posed challenges for the evaluators." Worse: "We also observed cases where Evaluator LLMs did not adjust their scores for perturbed responses despite correctly identifying the perturbations in their explanations." Giving the judge the gold answer helped: "reference-based evaluations showed comparatively better performance."
Construction: take a gold answer, have GPT-4-Turbo introduce one targeted defect, then have humans check it. 17 graduate volunteers labeled each perturbation Valid, Invalid, Score Invariant, Not Relevant or Not Sure; "Valid" only if "the perturbation warranted a score penalty relative to the gold answer."
Relevance: the exact failure the requester saw, measured at scale.
SOURCE: Doddapaneni, Khan, Verma, Khapra, "Finding Blind Spots in Evaluator LLMs with Interpretable Checklists" (FBI), 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13439 (quotes from abstract and full text, Appendix A)
COSTS: One strong model call per planted defect (about 100 to 500 calls), plus a human or second-model validity check on each. No GPU. The 17-person human check can be replaced by one person sampling (my adaptation).

SECTION 2. Sanity checks: a method that looks right can be provably independent of the thing it claims to measure
Adebayo et al. ran two negative controls on saliency-map explanation methods: randomize the model weights, or randomize the training labels. "If the saliency method depends on the learned parameters of the model, we should expect its output to differ substantially between the two cases. Should the outputs be similar, however, we can infer that the saliency map is insensitive to properties of the model." Result: "Of the methods tested, Gradients & GradCAM pass the sanity checks, while Guided BackProp & Guided GradCAM are invariant to higher layer parameters; hence, fail." Why it matters here: "reliance, solely, on visual assessment can be misleading" and "visual inspection is a poor guide in judging whether an explanation is sensitive to the underlying model and data."
Transfer to a quality checker: feed it output that has been scrambled, shuffled across tasks, or produced for a different task. If its verdict does not change, it is not reading the output. Same logic as the requester's four checkers that passed a known-broken file. (The transfer is my inference; the test design is the paper's.)
SOURCE: Adebayo, Gilmer, Muelly, Goodfellow, Hardt, Kim, "Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps", 2018, https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03292 (quotes from full text)
COSTS: Two extra runs of the checker on corrupted inputs. Minutes, no extra infrastructure.

SECTION 3. Null models: a constant, irrelevant answer can beat real answers on automatic judges
Zheng et al.: "even a 'null model' that always outputs a constant response (irrelevant to input instructions) can cheat automatic benchmarks and achieve top-ranked win rates: an 86.5% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0; an 83.0 score on Arena-Hard-Auto; and a 9.55 score on MT-Bench." This is the cheapest known-bad: an answer that is plainly wrong, fed to the judge. Raina et al. show the same family: "short universal adversarial phrases can be concatenated to deceive judge LLMs to predict inflated scores ... irrespective of the assessed text, maximum scores are predicted," and "judge-LLMs are significantly more susceptible to these adversarial attacks when used for absolute scoring, as opposed to comparative assessment."
Local checklist makes the same point as a default test: "run (a) an oracle ... and (b) a null baseline - empty output, a constant answer, or the majority class - through the whole pipeline ... if the null doesn't fail, the grader is too lenient."
SOURCE: Zheng, Pang, Du, Liu, Jiang, Lin, "Cheating Automatic LLM Benchmarks: Null Models Achieve High Win Rates", 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137 ; Raina, Liusie, Gales, "Is LLM-as-a-Judge Robust?", 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14016 ; repo: claude-api skill, file: shared/evals/eval-audit.md, section 2 ("Harness tested on known-good and known-bad")
COSTS: Empty string, constant string, off-topic answer: three judge calls per criterion. Near zero. The attack-phrase search in Raina needs a surrogate model and optimisation; skip it.

SECTION 4. Adversarial pairs: natural sets hide judge weakness, adversarial sets expose it
LLMBar (419 manually curated pairs) ships two sets. "The NATURAL set collects and filters preference data from existing benchmarks ... Conversely, the ADVERSARIAL set comprises adversarially crafted instances that tend to confound less adept evaluators." Result: "on the ADVERSARIAL set, ChatGPT-based, LLaMA-2-Chat-based, and Falcon-based evaluators show worse-than-chance performance; even the best-performing GPT-4-based evaluator has a significant gap from expert human annotators." The bad answer is built to look good: the dispreferred output has "superficial quality, e.g., with a more polished tone or a better format" but does not follow the instruction. JudgeBench builds bad answers from objective correctness (wrong answer to a hard question) and reports "many strong models (e.g., GPT-4o) performing just slightly better than random guessing." RewardBench: "comparison datasets ... that have subtle, but verifiable reasons (e.g. bugs, incorrect facts) why one answer should be preferred."
Lesson: obvious bad examples are passed by everything; the set must contain bad answers that look good.
SOURCE: Zeng, Yu, Gao, Meng, Goyal, Chen, "Evaluating Large Language Models at Evaluating Instruction Following" (LLMBar), 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07641 ; Tan et al., "JudgeBench", 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784 ; Lambert et al., "RewardBench", 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13787
COSTS: Building a polished-but-wrong answer is one generation call plus one human or strong-model check per pair; adversarial filtering needs a weak judge to run the candidates through (LLMBar used ChatGPT). Hundreds of calls, no GPU.

SECTION 5. CheckList: three hand-built test types, and what they found
Ribeiro et al. name the vocabulary. "A Minimum Functionality test (MFT) ... is a collection of simple examples (and labels) to check a behavior within a capability." "An Invariance test (INV) is when we apply label-preserving perturbations to inputs and expect the model prediction to remain the same." "A Directional Expectation test (DIR) is similar, except that the label is expected to change in a certain way." They note INV and DIR "test models on unlabeled data," because they check relations between predictions, not ground truth; the paper ties these to "software metamorphic tests (Segura et al., 2016)." Templates make many cases cheaply (their negation template "I {NEGATION} {POS_VERB} the {THING}."). Findings: failure rate 76.4% on one negation MFT for a commercial model; "NLP practitioners with CheckList created twice as many tests, and found almost three times as many bugs as users without it."
Stated limit: "CheckList cannot be directly used for non-behavioral issues such as ... labeling errors, annotator biases."
SOURCE: Ribeiro, Wu, Guestrin, Singh, "Beyond Accuracy: Behavioral Testing of NLP Models with CheckList", 2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04118
COSTS: One person to write the capability list and templates; a script to expand them. Hundreds of cases from a handful of templates. No models needed to build; one run of the checker to score.

SECTION 6. Contrast sets: minimally edit a good example until it becomes bad
Gardner et al.: "after a dataset is constructed, we recommend that the dataset authors manually perturb the test instances in small but meaningful ways that (typically) change the gold label, creating contrast sets." Effect: "Although our contrast sets are not explicitly adversarial, model performance is significantly lower on them than on the original test sets---up to 25% in some cases." Effort: "about a person-week of work can yield high-quality perturbed test sets of approximately 1000 instances." Their consistency metric asks whether the model is right on the original AND on every minimal edit around it.
Limits stated by the authors: expert contrast sets "can only give us an upper bound on a model's local alignment with the true decision boundary" and "post-hoc contrast sets may be biased by existing models."
For a checker: take one output the checker passes, break it in one small way, confirm the verdict flips.
SOURCE: Gardner et al., "Evaluating Models' Local Decision Boundaries via Contrast Sets", 2020, https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.02709
COSTS: Human time: roughly 1000 edited instances per person-week (their figure for experts). Cheaper version: model-generated single-defect edits plus a human check of a sample, as in section 1.

SECTION 7. Mutation testing: the software version of "plant a defect and see if the check notices"
Mechanism: copy the program, change it slightly (a mutant), run the test suite. Stryker: "When at least one test failed while this mutant was active, the mutant is killed." "When all tests passed while this mutant was active, the mutant survived." Score: "detected / valid * 100." Wikipedia: "the mutation score is the number of mutants killed / total number of mutants"; "Mutation testing was originally proposed by Richard Lipton as a student in 1971, and first developed and published by DeMillo, Lipton and Sayward." Typical operators (mutmut): "Integer literals are changed by adding 1 ... `<` is changed to `<=` break is changed to continue." Cosmic Ray: "It makes small changes to your source code, running your test suite for each one."
Why it works (assumption): the competent programmer hypothesis, "most programmers are competent enough to create correct or almost correct source code" (Ahmed et al.) -- so real bugs are near the correct program.
NOT FOUND: a direct quote from DeMillo, Lipton, Sayward 1978 (IEEE Computer 11(4):34-41). The PDF mirror returned 403 and Semantic Scholar returned 429; I did not cite it from memory. Mutmut and Cosmic Ray READMEs fetched contain nothing on equivalent mutants.
Direct use: the tool is the checker, the mutants are corrupted outputs. Mutation score of a checker = fraction of planted defects it catches.
SOURCE: https://stryker-mutator.io/docs/mutation-testing-elements/mutant-states-and-metrics/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation_testing ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/boxed/mutmut/main/README.rst ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sixty-north/cosmic-ray/master/README.rst ; Ahmed et al., 2021, https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02517
COSTS: For code: mutmut/Cosmic Ray/Stryker run on one machine; cost is test-suite runtime times mutant count. For prose or files: scripted or model-written corruptions, one checker run each.

SECTION 8. Equivalent mutants: the known way a bad set lies to you (a planted "defect" that is not a defect)
Wikipedia: "Equivalent mutants detection is one of the biggest obstacles to practical usage of mutation testing." Google (Petrovic et al.): "Equivalent mutants, which are semantically equivalent to the mutated program, are a plague in mutation testing and cannot generally be detected automatically." Even after sampling, "developers at Google initially classified 85% of reported mutants as unproductive. An unproductive mutant is either trivially equivalent to the original program or it is detectable, but adding a test for it would not improve the test suite." Also: "equivalent mutants can be productive."
The same failure appears in the LLM-judge version, and people have counted it. FBI needed humans to mark "Score Invariant" perturbations (section 1). Delcourt et al. manually inspected 79 hard cases: "69 were confirmed as valid degradations, while 10 were borderline mutants for which the transformation had little negative effect ... which may underestimate the kill ratio of a competent judge." Mubric adds a verifier: "checks whether the intended defect is present and whether it reduces response quality."
Consequence: when the checker "misses" a planted defect, first ask whether the defect is real. Check every survivor by hand before blaming the checker.
SOURCE: Petrovic, Ivankovic, Fraser, Just, "Practical Mutation Testing at Scale", 2021, https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11378 (full text) ; Delcourt et al., 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14315 ; Yang et al., "Mubric", 2026, https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37322
COSTS: Reading each surviving planted defect: minutes each. Automatic equivalence detection is undecidable in general (Petrovic quote), so a human or strong-model pass is the cheap version.

SECTION 9. Planted defects are not the real defects: coverage gap between mutants and real faults
Just et al. (357 real faults, 5 Java programs): "the coupling effect exists for 73% of the real faults"; "17% of faults are not coupled to any mutants, which reveals a fundamental limitation of mutation analysis"; "10% of real faults require a new or stronger mutation operator." Mutant detection still correlated with real-fault detection "even if code coverage is controlled." Ahmed et al.: "mutation testing is missing important operators to generate representative real-world bugs." Petrovic et al. (15 million mutants): "mutants are indeed coupled with real faults."
Transfer: a known-bad set made from defects you imagined covers about what you imagined. Source the defects from real past failures (Mubric builds operators from "real preferred-dispreferred response pairs"; FBI/LLMBar build from known failure types), and keep adding new real failures as they appear.
SOURCE: Just, Jalali, Inozemtseva, Ernst, Holmes, Fraser, "Are Mutants a Valid Substitute for Real Faults in Software Testing?", FSE 2014, https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~rjust/publ/mutants_real_faults_fse_2014.pdf ; Ahmed et al. https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02517 ; Petrovic et al. "Does mutation testing improve testing practices?" https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07189
COSTS: Needs a log of real past failures. Reading them is human time; no compute.

SECTION 10. Mutation testing already applied to LLM judges, with a measured agreement against humans
Delcourt et al.: "A candidate judge is then evaluated based on its ability to detect the injected defects. We define 11 mutation operators ... Across six judge configurations (three LLMs and two prompt variants), the automated mutation testing approach is largely consistent with the manual assessment in identifying the better-performing configurations." Numbers: "manual precision and mutation kill ratio have a Pearson correlation of r = 0.624"; "the two instruments produce the same ordering for 11 pairs (agreement rate of 73.3%)." Overall kill ratio 82.4%; aggregate hides differences: "Aggregate kill ratios conceal substantial differences among defect categories," so report kill rate per operator. Caveats: reference built on 60 judgments, annotator agreement "Krippendorff's alpha = 0.32." One domain only (PlantUML class diagrams).
Mubric uses the same idea to improve rubrics: "If an injected defect causes little or no score reduction, Mubric interprets this as evidence that the corresponding quality requirement is insufficiently captured by the rubric."
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14315 (Delcourt, Ben Chaaben, Rouatbi, Marchezan, Sahraoui, 2026) ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37322 (Yang, Zhang, Lou, Chen, 2026)
COSTS: Operators written once (11 in Delcourt; Mubric derived 201 from real response pairs). Per judge configuration: one judge call per mutant. Cheap-version kill detection used sentence-embedding similarity (F1 0.90); a strong-model check is the alternative.

SECTION 11. A known-bad set alone is gameable: the judge that flags everything kills every mutant
Delcourt et al.: "reporting many possible issues increases the probability of matching an injected defect and can therefore increase the kill ratio, even when many of the additional issues are invalid." Example: one model "frequently detected injected defects (high kill ratio), while also reporting many unsupported issues on non-mutated diagrams (low manual precision)." The mirror of the requester's problem: the requester's checkers passed everything; a checker that fails everything also scores perfectly on bad examples.
Practitioner statement of the same: "if errors occur in only 5% of examples, a judge that always predicts Pass still has 95% agreement while detecting none of the errors"; measure "true positive rate and true negative rate" (Husain; quotes as returned by page fetch). Local checklist: "When both positives and negatives exist, prefer precision/recall/specificity to accuracy alone" and "Both-directions coverage."
Rule: every checker run needs matched known-good AND known-bad, reported as two numbers.
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14315 ; https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/ (Hamel Husain, blog, practitioner source not peer-reviewed) ; repo: claude-api skill, file: shared/evals/eval-audit.md, section 1 ("Class balance", "Both-directions coverage")
COSTS: Known-good set of equal size as bad set; one extra checker pass.

SECTION 12. How many bad examples? What the sources actually give
NOT FOUND: a published minimum count of known-bad examples for a judge, and a published curve of judge reliability against number of failure cases. I searched and found none; do not claim one exists.
What can be sourced:
(a) Statistics. Rule of three: "If a certain event did not occur in a sample with n subjects, the interval from 0 to 3/n is a 95% confidence interval for the rate of occurrences in the population" (Hanley and Lippman-Hand, JAMA 1983, via Wikipedia). My arithmetic from it: if a checker catches all n planted defects, its true miss rate could still be up to 3/n: n=10 gives up to 30%; n=30 gives up to 10%. One miss, by contrast, proves the checker fails on that class of defect (existence proof; my inference, once the defect is verified real per section 8).
(b) Practitioner: "Aim for 30 to 50 examples of each label in each set when possible" (Husain, blog).
(c) Local: noise floor "for a pass-rate, roughly 1/sqrt(n*R): 25 cases x 2 reps ~ +/-14 points, 100 x 2 ~ +/-7."
(d) Scale in published work: FBI 2400 over 22 categories (about 109 per category); Gardner about 1000 instances per dataset.
(e) Miller: 95% CI is "mean +/- 1.96 x SE"; also gives sample-size formulas for planning.
SOURCE: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(statistics) ; https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/ ; repo: claude-api skill, file: shared/evals/eval-audit.md, section 5 ; Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals", 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640
COSTS: Arithmetic only. Practical target from these sources: at least 30 verified bad examples per defect class gives a 10% worst-case miss rate if all are caught.

SECTION 13. Negative controls (experimental design) and what they cannot catch
Epidemiology term. IJE guidance: a negative control is "A variable that (i) is not causally associated with the exposure (for an NCO) or the outcome (for an NCE) of interest and (ii) shares its bias structure with the exposure ... or outcome ... of interest." Used to detect confounding and bias: if the method reports an effect where none can exist, the method is biased. Stated limits: "Negative controls are, in some instances, poor at detecting biases that move estimates toward the null, such as misclassification. Positive controls may be a more effective tool for those situations." And: "the choice of a negative control is not straightforward."
Transfer: negative control = input where the right verdict is known to be FAIL for a reason unrelated to what the checker claims to read; positive control = input that must pass. A checker biased toward saying "pass" needs negatives; one biased toward "fail" needs positives.
NOT FOUND: the original Lipsitch, Tchetgen Tchetgen, Cohen (2010) text; my one fetch of the PMC link returned an unrelated article. Not quoted.
SOURCE: "Negative controls and how to use them: a guidance paper", Int J Epidemiology vol 55(5), https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/55/5/dyag163/8776067
COSTS: Design time only.

SECTION 14. Where known-bad sets mislead: five documented ways
1. Set built against one model favors that model's family. LLMBar: "since the adversarial outputs are created by GPT-4, GPT-4-based evaluators may have an unfair advantage when they are assessed on this subset."
2. Post-hoc sets inherit the bias of what was tried. Gardner: "post-hoc contrast sets may be biased by existing models."
3. Defects that are not defects (section 8).
4. Defects you imagine are not the defects that occur (section 9: 17% of real faults coupled to no mutant).
5. Label noise: Delcourt's initial annotator agreement was Krippendorff's alpha = 0.32, so even "ground truth" about what counts as bad was unstable; LLMBar reports expert agreement of 94% by selecting for it ("expert annotator agreement rate of 94%").
Also: pass/fail on a bad set is a lower-bound test only. Passing it does not prove the checker is good; failing it proves the checker is broken (Adebayo's framing: tests that "can safely reject the method").
SOURCE: https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07641 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.02709 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14315 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11378 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03292
COSTS: Mitigation is process: mixed-origin bad examples (hand-written, different model families, real past failures) and a second reader on each.

SECTION 15. Local checklist: the concrete known-bad steps already written down
repo: claude-api skill, file: shared/evals/eval-audit.md. Quotes:
"Harness tested on known-good and known-bad. Before the first full pass, run (a) an oracle ... and (b) a null baseline ... If the oracle doesn't pass, the harness or grader is broken; if the null doesn't fail, the grader is too lenient. Two runs, minutes, and it catches most wiring bugs."
"Not too lenient. ... Write a deliberately wrong-but-plausible answer and confirm it fails."
"Tested on known negatives. Feed the judge an empty string, 'I don't know', and a confident answer to the wrong question; confirm it fails all three."
"Calibrated against human labels. Validate the judge on a few dozen cases a human labelled independently ... Well below ~90% on clear-cut cases means the judge prompt needs another iteration."
"Inverted items as a smoke test. Where a clearly weaker variant outscores a clearly stronger one on an item, it is far more often a case or grader bug than a real inversion."
"Spot-check the failures. ... If more than roughly one in ten look like grader errors, fix the grader before any full pass."
"Deterministic, or with measured variance. Run the grader on the same output twice."
Also build-eval.md step: "confirm an induced API error lands as status: error, not grade: 0" (a checker error counted as a pass or zero is a known-bad of the harness itself).
SOURCE: repo: claude-api skill, files: shared/evals/eval-audit.md (sections 1, 2, 4) and shared/evals/build-eval.md (line on running eval-audit before first pass)
COSTS: A few judge calls and about an hour; no humans beyond one reader for the "few dozen" calibration labels.
