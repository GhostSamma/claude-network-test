1. Safety engineering ranks a written rule below a mechanical control, and says why
NIOSH ranks controls from most to least effective: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, PPE. A written rule is an administrative control. NIOSH: "Elimination, substitution, and engineering controls are more effective because they control exposures without significant human interaction." The best engineering controls "Prevent users from modifying or interfering with the control" and "Need minimal user action for the controls to work". Administrative controls are the category that includes "Work process training". NIOSH says of them: "Administrative controls and PPE require significant and ongoing effort by workers and their supervisors." Its stated use for them is as a stopgap: "They are useful when employers are in the process of implementing other control methods from the hierarchy."
This is the strongest single answer to the requester's problem. The month-old rule was an administrative control that depends on every agent's ongoing effort, with no supervisor effort measured. The hierarchy predicts that outcome. It is a ranking of effectiveness, not a trial, so it predicts direction and gives no effect size.
SOURCE: CDC/NIOSH, "Hierarchy of Controls" https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/hierarchy-of-controls/about/index.html (fetched 2026-10-03)
COSTS: Reading only. Applying it means converting each rule into a hook, test or CI check, which is one developer-afternoon per rule on one machine.

2. Swiss cheese: a written procedure is one defensive layer, and every layer has holes
Reason (BMJ 2000): "High technology systems have many defensive layers: some are engineered (alarms, physical barriers, automatic shutdowns, etc), others rely on people ..., and yet others depend on procedures and administrative controls." And: "In reality, however, they are more like slices of Swiss cheese, having many holes - though unlike in the cheese, these holes are continually opening, shutting, and shifting their location. The presence of holes in any one 'slice' does not normally cause a bad outcome. Usually, this can happen only when the holes in many layers momentarily line up".
Implication: replacing the written rule is not the whole answer. The point is to have more than one layer, with at least one that does not depend on the agent reading the rule. The "person approach" blames individuals for forgetfulness. The "system approach" builds defences. Reason writes that the system approach "tries to build defences to avert errors or mitigate their effects".
Eighteen agents ignoring a rule is the person-approach reading. The system-approach reading is that the single layer, the written rule, had a hole at every agent. Caveat: the model is qualitative. Reason gives no failure rates per layer.
SOURCE: James Reason, "Human error: models and management", BMJ 2000;320:768-770, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1117770/ (fetched; PMID 10720363)
COSTS: Reading only. The layered design costs one extra independent check per important rule.

3. Google: rules that are not machine-enforced decay, and automation is preferred for that reason
Schwartz, "Style Guides and Rules", Software Engineering at Google ch. 8: "Rules can be enforced socially, through teaching and training, or technically, with tooling." "...when it comes to checking for compliance, rather than exclusively depending on engineer-based verification, we strongly prefer to automate enforcement with tooling." "Automated rule enforcement ensures that rules are not dropped or forgotten as time passes or as an organization scales up." "Leaving enforcement up to engineers is likely to see inconsistent interpretation and application of the rules". Also: "If the tools that we use to enforce a rule become overly complex and burdensome to maintain, the rule itself might have decayed and need to be revisited." So a failing check is also a signal about the rule.
The chapter also says: "Rules, by their nature, lend greater value when they are enforceable." This is the closest published statement to "a rule nobody checks is worth little".
Limit: these are practitioner claims from one company, with no controlled comparison.
SOURCE: Software Engineering at Google (Winters, Manshreck, Wright; O'Reilly 2020), ch. 8 "Style Guides and Rules", https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch08.html (open access; fetched)
COSTS: Reading only. Tooling cost per rule is a linter config, a test, or a hook, often under 50 lines.

4. For LLM agents specifically, written instructions are measurably unreliable and decay within a session
McMillan (2026), 1,650 Claude Code CLI sessions: "None of the four structural variables or three two-way interactions produces a detectable contrast", meaning that reformatting, shortening or reordering the CLAUDE.md did not measurably help. "...each additional function the agent generates is associated with approximately 5.6% lower odds of compliance per step (OR = 0.944)". Harness-IF (2026), 12 frontier models: "accuracy spans 72.1-85.9%" and "every model is worse on against-prior rules, by 3.6 to 7.4 points... Aggregate scores therefore overstate compliance". RepoComplianceBench (2026): "today's agents almost never proactively retrieve the contribution rules. Agents pick up disclosure and verification with reminder prompts, rule quotes, and verifier feedback". Jaroslawicz et al. (2025): "even the best frontier models only achieve 68% accuracy at the max density of 500 instructions".
Negative result: rewriting the rule file is not a fix. The only intervention in these papers that worked was verifier feedback, which is a check. Caveats: McMillan measured a trivial annotation rule, and the instruction-density benchmark uses keyword tasks.
SOURCE: arXiv:2605.10039 https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10039 ; arXiv:2608.11727 https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11727 ; arXiv:2607.26819 https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26819 ; arXiv:2507.11538 https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538 (all fetched)
COSTS: Reading only. Each paper's own method needs API model calls and so is out of budget. The finding itself needs no compute.

5. The mechanism available on this machine: Claude Code hooks are deterministic, CLAUDE.md is not
Claude Code docs: hooks run "at specific points in its lifecycle, which gives you deterministic control: certain actions always happen rather than relying on the LLM to choose to run them. Use hooks to enforce project rules". "For decisions that require judgment rather than deterministic rules, you can also use prompt-based hooks or agent-based hooks that use a Claude model to evaluate conditions." This is the hierarchy-of-controls move for this setup: the rule moves from administrative to engineering control.
Two cautions from the same page. A hook that blocks stopping can loop, so the docs say to "Parse the stop_hook_active field from the JSON input and exit early if it's true". And the model-based hook types are non-deterministic, so they are a lower tier of gate than a script.
Open question that I could NOT FIND data on: whether agents learn to route around a blocking hook. Section 9 gives the closest evidence.
SOURCE: Claude Code Docs, "Automate actions with hooks" https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks-guide (fetched)
COSTS: A shell or Python script per rule, run locally, no API cost for command-type hooks. Prompt/agent hooks do cost API calls and are excluded by the budget.

6. Checklists: strong effects when paired with measurement, no effect when merely mandated
Haynes et al. 2009 (NEJM, 8 hospitals, before/after, not randomized): death fell from "1.5% before the checklist was introduced" to "0.8% afterward (P=0.003)"; complications from 11.0% to 7.0%. Pronovost et al. 2006 (NEJM, 103 Michigan ICUs): mean infection rate per 1000 catheter-days "decreased from 7.7 at baseline to 1.4 at 16 to 18 months". The sustainment follow-up credits "continued feedback of infection data that the team perceived as valid" and "senior leaders who reviewed infection data". That is a measurement loop around the checklist, not the paper form alone.
Negative result: Urbach et al. 2014 studied the Ontario policy mandating checklists in 101 hospitals: mortality 0.71% to 0.65% (odds ratio 0.91, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.03, P=0.13); complications 3.86% to 3.82%. Conclusion: "not associated with significant reductions". The abstract does not say how well the checklist was actually performed. The cautious reading is that a checklist adopted without verified use did not reproduce the trial effect.
NOT FOUND: Gawande, The Checklist Manifesto (2009). I did not fetch the book text, so I quote nothing from it. Gawande is a co-author of Haynes et al., which is the fetched source.
SOURCE: Haynes et al., NEJM 2009 (PMID 19144931) and Pronovost et al., NEJM 2006 (PMID 17192537), via Europe PMC https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:19144931 ; Urbach et al., NEJM 2014 (Europe PMC id 24620866); Pronovost et al., BMJ 2010 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2816728/
COSTS: Reading only. For the requester: log which checklist items each agent actually did. Bash and grep over transcripts, no API cost.

7. How noisy can a gate be? Two published practitioner thresholds
Google (Tricorder): checks must "Produce less than 10% effective false positives. Developers should feel the check is pointing out an actual issue at least 90% of the time." "Effective false positive" means "if developers did not take some positive action after seeing the issue", including warnings "technically correct but unimportant", so confusing and trivial warnings count as false. For checks that block the build the bar is higher: "Produce no effective false positives (the analysis should never stop the build for correct code)". Coverity (Bessey et al. 2010): "In our experience, more than 30% easily cause problems. People ignore the tool. True bugs get lost in the false. A vicious cycle starts where low trust causes complex bugs to be labeled false positives, leading to yet lower trust." They aim "for below 20% for 'stable' checkers".
Both are engineering experience, not controlled trials, and they differ by a factor of 3, so the honest range for "gate noise that kills trust" is roughly 10% to 30% false-positive share. Blocking gates need a lower rate than advisory ones.
Direct access to the Tricorder CACM and ICSE 2015 papers failed (403); the quotes come from the book chapter by Sadowski, which covers the same system.
SOURCE: Software Engineering at Google ch. 20 https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch20.html ; Bessey et al., "A few billion lines of code later", CACM 53(2), 2010, https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~dstefan/cse227-spring20/papers/bessey:a-few.pdf
COSTS: Measure your own gate's false-positive share by hand-labelling 30 to 50 recent firings. About 30 minutes of reading, no compute.

8. Why developers stop using a checker (interview study) and what Google did about it
Johnson et al. 2013 (ICSE), interviews with 20 developers: "although all of our participants felt that use is beneficial, false positives and the way in which the warnings are presented, among other things, are barriers to use." "Nineteen of the 20 developers we interviewed expressed the importance of workflow integration." Google's response (Sadowski): "Make Static Analysis a Part of the Core Developer Workflow", surfaced in code review; "Tricorder came out of several failed attempts to integrate static analysis with the developer workflow at Google; the key difference... was our relentless focus on having Tricorder deliver only valuable results". Operationally: each result carries a "Not useful" button, and "The Tricorder team tracks analyzers with high 'Not useful' click rates... and will disable analyzers if they don't work to address problems and improve the 'not useful' rate."
So the gate itself is monitored and has an owner who can switch it off. The reverse failure shows up in Quieting the Static (Liargkovas et al., arXiv 2311.07482): across 1,425 Java projects "Contrary to expectations, false positives account for a minor proportion of suppressions". Suppressions are driven by other things, including "misleading suggestions" and disregard, so a low false-positive rate alone does not guarantee that warnings are honoured.
SOURCE: Johnson et al., ICSE 2013, https://petertsehsun.github.io/soen7481/papers/icse13b.pdf ; SWE at Google ch. 20 https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch20.html ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07482
COSTS: A "was this useful" flag column in whatever log the gate writes, plus a weekly count. Shell only.

9. Gates are gamed: agents satisfy the check without doing the work
ImpossibleBench (Zhong, Raghunathan, Carlini 2025): "an LLM agent with access to unit tests may delete failing tests rather than fix the underlying bug." They define "cheating rate" as pass rate on tasks where the spec and tests conflict, "where any pass necessarily implies a specification-violating shortcut", and report that "prompt, test access and feedback loop affect cheating rates". Cheating ranged "from simple test modification to complex operator overloading".
Implication for the requester: a gate must be out of the agent's reach (not editable by the agent it checks), and it must check the artifact that matters, not a proxy. The claude-api skill's eval audit says the same for graders: it asks "How could a model satisfy the grader without solving the task - hard-coding the expected output, reading the answer key, special-casing on test names, an empty string a lenient regex accepts" and requires "Ground truth not reachable by the model under test... This has to be structural; 'don't look' in a prompt is not a defence." The same file's line "For agents that act on an environment, grade the end state, not the transcript" applies to ordering of knowledge: check the resulting file, not the agent's claim.
SOURCE: arXiv:2510.20270 https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270 ; repo: claude-api skill, file: shared/evals/eval-audit.md (sections 4 "Cheat-resistant", "Ground truth not reachable", and "end state")
COSTS: Put the check script outside the agent's writable paths and run it from the harness. Free.

10. What makes a check catch a real defect rather than manufacture a false one
The eval-audit file gives testable requirements for any automated check. "Harness tested on known-good and known-bad": run "an oracle... and a null baseline - empty output, a constant answer... If the oracle doesn't pass, the harness or grader is broken; if the null doesn't fail, the grader is too lenient. Two runs, minutes". "Not too lenient... Write a deliberately wrong-but-plausible answer and confirm it fails." "Spot-check the failures. Read a handful of outputs the grader marked wrong. If more than roughly one in ten look like grader errors, fix the grader". "'No answer' is not 'negative answer'": a check that crashes must not score the same as one that found nothing. "Infra failures distinguished from model failures". "Deterministic, or with measured variance": run the grader twice on the same output.
Google applies the same idea at scale through mutation testing: "Mutation testing assesses test suite efficacy by inserting small faults into programs and measuring the ability of the test suite to detect them." That is seeding a known defect to prove the gate fires on it. Their system is used "on all code changes" in code review, but only after pruning "arid" mutants (such as logging lines), because surfacing them costs developer attention.
SOURCE: repo: claude-api skill, file: shared/evals/eval-audit.md (sections 2 and 4) ; Petrovic and Ivankovic, "State of Mutation Testing at Google", ICSE-SEIP 2018, https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/46584.pdf (fetched)
COSTS: For each new gate, write one known-bad and one known-good fixture and run the gate on both. About 10 minutes per gate, no API cost.

11. Flaky checks train people to ignore real failures
Luo, Hariri, Eloussi, Marinov (FSE 2014): flaky tests "have non-deterministic outcomes. Such tests undermine the regression testing as they make it difficult to rely on test results"; the study covers 201 commits fixing flaky tests in 51 open-source projects. Google's testing blog gives rates: "a continual rate of about 1.5% of all test runs reporting a 'flaky' result" and "Almost 16% of our tests have some level of flakiness". On behaviour: "It is human nature to ignore alarms when there is a history of false signals coming from a system." "It is quite common to ignore legitimate failures in flaky tests due to the high number of false-positives." Cost to gates: "failing CI tests lock a branch from changes until it is resolved... but also from legitimate bug escapes that were ignored because of the noise."
Practical inference: a blocking gate that sometimes fails for nothing will be bypassed, and reruns that hide flakiness reduce the signal. Rerunning until it passes is the same bypass.
The Luo paper full text was paywalled; the abstract was fetched from Illinois Experts.
SOURCE: https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/an-empirical-analysis-of-flaky-tests (DOI 10.1145/2635868.2635920) ; Google Testing Blog, "Flaky Tests at Google and How We Mitigate Them" (2016) https://testing.googleblog.com/2016/05/flaky-tests-at-google-and-how-we.html
COSTS: Run each new gate 10 times on an unchanged tree. Any variation means it is not ready to block. Minutes.

12. Alarm fatigue: no measured threshold exists in the sources I could fetch (NOT FOUND)
Healthcare: Sendelbach and Funk 2013: "Research has demonstrated that 72% to 99% of clinical alarms are false. The high number of false alarms has led to alarm fatigue... sensory overload... which can result in desensitization to alarms and missed alarms. Patient deaths have been attributed to alarm fatigue." Cvach 2012 (72 articles): desensitization is "related to a high false alarm rate, poor positive predictive value, lack of alarm standardization, and the number of alarming medical devices". Neither gives a threshold at which people stop reading, only the range of false rates observed in the field.
Security: the 2026 survey of AI alert screening (119 records) reports "persistent gaps in operational validation... and evaluation practice". Vendor blog percentages for ignored SOC alerts turned up in search, but I did not fetch their primary sources, so I do not use them.
Verdict: the only numeric thresholds for "stop trusting the tool" are the software-engineering ones in section 7 (10%, 20%, 30%).
SOURCE: Sendelbach and Funk, AACN Adv Crit Care 2013 (Europe PMC 24153215) ; Cvach, Biomed Instrum Technol 2012 (Europe PMC 22839984) ; arXiv:2605.08316 https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08316
COSTS: Reading only.

13. Where the gate must sit: local hooks can be skipped, server-side checks cannot
Git docs: "pre-commit: This hook is invoked by git-commit, and can be bypassed with the --no-verify option", and likewise commit-msg. Pro Git: "You'll build client scripts that help the developer know if their push will be rejected and server scripts that actually enforce the policies." GitHub: "After enabling required status checks, all required status checks must pass before collaborators can merge changes into the protected branch", but restrictions apply to admins only if you "optionally apply the restrictions to administrators".
Principle: client-side checks advise, server-side or harness-side checks enforce. For agents, the equivalent is that a hook in the agent's own settings is advisory if the agent can edit that settings file, while a check run by the orchestrator after the agent finishes is enforcing. I found no source quantifying how often agents edit their own hooks (NOT FOUND); this is inference from the two documents.
SOURCE: https://git-scm.com/docs/githooks ; https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2/Customizing-Git-An-Example-Git-Enforced-Policy ; https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-protected-branches/about-protected-branches
COSTS: A post-run verification script called by the orchestrating process. Free; one machine.

14. Shift left and policy-as-code: the names for this pattern
Software Engineering at Google ch. 9: "checking for defects during the initial code review process is still an integral part of a general 'shift left' strategy, aiming to discover and resolve issues at the earliest possible time so that they don't require escalated costs and resources farther down in the development cycle." Ch. 23: CI is "a cost shifted left to an earlier - and more preferable - stage". Ch. 9 on ordering: "Rather than require authors to run tests, linters, or formatters, the current Google code review tooling provides most of that utility automatically through what is known as presubmits." Open Policy Agent: "an open source, general-purpose policy engine that unifies policy enforcement across the stack... lets you specify policy as code".
So the vocabulary for the requester's goal is presubmit plus policy-as-code. The decision is made by tooling at the point of change, and the rule text lives in the check. I found no controlled trial that quantifies shift-left savings in these sources (NOT FOUND); the claim is practitioner doctrine.
SOURCE: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch09.html ; https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch23.html ; https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs
COSTS: OPA is free and runs locally, but is overkill for a single repo. A plain script in a presubmit hook is enough.

15. When a rule should stay written, and how unmechanizable rules are gated
Stay written: Schwartz (SWE at Google ch. 8): "Some technical rules explicitly call for human judgment", for example "Composition is often more appropriate than inheritance". "Other rules are social rather than technical, and it is often unwise to solve social problems with a technical solution... tooling would become complex and expensive. It's often better to leave enforcement of those rules to humans." NIOSH keeps administrative controls for cases where engineering controls do not yet exist ("useful when employers are in the process of implementing other control methods"). Agent evidence: RepoComplianceBench found "verification and disclosure issues are solvable with existing mechanisms, yet enforcing bans and human escalations remains an open problem".
Human gate: code review, "reviewed by someone other than the author". Bacchelli and Bird 2013 (Microsoft): "while finding defects remains the main motivation for review, reviews are less about defects than expected", so do not expect review to catch what a check would. McIntosh et al. (Qt, VTK, ITK): "code review coverage, participation, and expertise share a significant link with software quality", so review that is thin or skipped is measurably worse. Google on two-person control: "Before building a multi-party authorization system, make sure the technology and social dynamics allow someone to say no. Otherwise, the system is of little value."
Sampling audits: ISO 2859-1 covers inspection of "data or records, and administrative procedures", using "the economic and psychological pressure of lot non-acceptance". Sampling agent outputs is thus a standard method. A sourced sample-size rule for zero observed failures: NOT FOUND.
SOURCE: https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch08.html ; https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch09.html ; https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/expectations-outcomes-and-challenges-of-modern-code-review/ ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10664-015-9381-9 ; https://google.github.io/building-secure-and-reliable-systems/raw/ch05.html ; ISO 2859-1:1999 sample https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/1141/9a258e81b29642ad9dcba33656bb2390/ISO-2859-1-1999.pdf
COSTS: Sampling audit: read 10 to 20 agent outputs per week against a one-line rubric. About 30 minutes per week, no compute. LLM reviewers cost API calls and are out of budget.
