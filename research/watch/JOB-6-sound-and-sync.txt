CUT-TO-BEAT 1: cuts on the accented beat vs between beats raised pleasure ratings, and viewers did not notice

Lin, Yeh and Shams (UCLA) showed the same video with the same music in two versions. In one, scene cuts landed on the accented first beat of a measure. In the other, the cuts were shifted to fall "a little less than halfway between the first beats of two consecutive measures". Abstract: "Despite the subliminal nature of this manipulation, a higher perceptual pleasure was found for temporal congruency compared with incongruency."

Numbers from the preprint full text. Experiment 1: the text says 143 participants, but later says "out of 145", so the n is inconsistent in the preprint. Six 30-40 s clips of Los Angeles time-lapses, between-subjects. "t=2.07, p<.05, Cohen's d=0.33". Experiment 2: 30 undergraduates, 30 varied clips of 30 s with cuts 2-3 s apart, within-subject. "t (29) = 2.16, p = 0.019, Cohen's dz=0.395". "With one exception, none of the participants reported noticing a difference... The one participant... had extensive experience in video editing."

What it does NOT show: the only outcome is a pleasure rating. The authors say "the measurements in the present study were restricted to subjective reports of pleasure rating". There is no watch time, no swipe-away, no memory. Participants were psychology undergraduates, and Experiment 2 ran online. The effect is small to medium (about a third of a standard deviation).

Editor control: fully. Where the cut falls relative to the beat is the editor's decision.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Lin, Yeh, Shams, "Subliminal audio-visual temporal congruency in music videos enhances perceptual pleasure", Neuroscience Letters 2022. Abstract confirmed at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1016/j.neulet.2022.136623&format=json&resultType=core (PMID 35398533). Numbers and quotes are from the preprint PDF (OSF/PsyArXiv dsg45, 2021): https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/dsg45 . The published version may differ from the preprint.
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Yes. Video and audio were identical across conditions. Only the timing offset between cuts and beats changed, done by sliding the soundtrack relative to the video. Content, music, cut count and pace were held constant. Clips and version varied across participants (Experiment 1) or within participants (Experiment 2). Limit: outcome is a rating, not viewing behavior.

MUSIC 1: meta-analysis of background music finds a null overall effect, a small negative effect on ad memory, a positive effect on emotion

Kämpfe, Sedlmeier and Renkewitz (2010) pooled lab studies of music vs no music. Abstract: "A global analysis shows a null effect"; "background music disturbs the reading process, has some small detrimental effects on memory, but has a positive impact on emotional reactions and improves achievements in sports."

Advertising subset: "Seven studies examined the impact of background music on how well potential customers memorized the contents of advertisements... Four of these studies used the radio as a medium... two employed TV commercials... and one used mobile message advertisements". "the effect over all seven studies was slightly negative but negligibly small (r-weighted = -.05, N = 7 studies, n = 930, rmin = -.22, rmax = .46)". Excluding the mobile study: "r-weighted = -.08, N = 6 studies". The authors also note that studies are scarce and of uneven quality: "effort should be made to develop more specific theories... and to increase the methodological quality of relevant studies."

Read plainly: across the studies they could pool, adding music did not help people remember ad content. If anything it hurt slightly. It did tend to help emotional reaction. None of the pooled studies is short vertical video, and the youngest is from 2006. No other music-in-advertising meta-analysis was found in this session. A search returned a claim attributed to Allan (2007), but I could not fetch it.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Kämpfe, Sedlmeier, Renkewitz, "The impact of background music on adult listeners: A meta-analysis", Psychology of Music, 2010 (Crossref issued date 2010-11-08). Full text at https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/music/distraction/2010-kampfe.pdf ; abstract at DOI 10.1177/0305735610376261
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Yes for music vs no music: pooled studies compare the same material with and without music. But they are lab studies, mostly radio and TV, with no editing manipulated, and no measure of whether people kept watching.

AUDIO EVENTS 1: sudden audio changes trigger an orienting response and better recognition of what follows (radio, not video)

Potter, Lang and Bolls (2008): "This study tested the ability of nine different auditory structural features to elicit orienting responses from radio listeners. It further tested the effect of the orienting response on listeners memory for information presented immediately following the orienting-eliciting structural feature. Results show that listeners do have significant decelerating cardiac patterns suggestive of orienting for eight of the nine features. Taken as a categorical whole, these features also increase recognition memory for the information presented after their onset compared to information presented immediately before."

The abstract does not name the nine features, and I could not fetch the full text, so I cannot list which eight worked or which one failed. Heart-rate deceleration is the measure of orienting. The memory effect is recognition memory for items right after the audio event.

Caveats: the medium is radio and the sample sizes are not available to me (abstract-only). Whether silence is among the nine features is not stated in the abstract. An audio change (voice change, sound effect, music sting) is an edit decision, so this is the nearest measured link between audio editing and attention. It has not been tested on short-form video.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Potter, Lang, Bolls, "Identifying Structural Features of Audio: Orienting Responses During Radio Messages and Their Impact on Recognition", Journal of Media Psychology 2008, abstract-only via Crossref API (DOI 10.1027/1864-1105.20.4.168): https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Identifying+Structural+Features+of+Audio
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Partly. Memory for items just after a feature's onset is compared with memory for items just before it, inside the same listeners and messages. That isolates the audio event from content. But the study is radio, lab, and the abstract does not say how content was balanced across positions.

AUDIO EVENTS 2: the orienting response to repeated audio events habituates in some conditions and not others

Potter, Sites, Jamison-Koenig and Zheng (2018): "Structural features such as voice changes, jingle onsets, and production effects in a radio broadcast elicit cardiac orienting responses." The paper summarizes earlier work (Potter et al. 2015): "listeners habituated to onsets of jingles and production effects" in passive listening, "unlike voice changes". And "Potter (2000) demonstrated that orienting to voice changes in audio messages failed to habituate after six occurrences in 2-minute radio dialogs."

New in this paper: when listeners were doing a task, "robust orienting to all three repetitions of the structural features in both the Hard and the Easy trials". Study 1 n=27, Study 2 n=59. The authors say heart rate may be insensitive to load effects, and the radio stimulus allowed only three repetitions per feature.

For the editor: repeating the same sting or sound effect is expected to wear off when the viewer is only listening. A voice change (a different speaker) is the audio event that did not wear off. When viewers are doing something else, the effect held across three repeats. This comes from radio with heart-rate measures. It says nothing about viewers swiping away. This paper reports no memory data: "Those data are not reported here."

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Potter, Sites, Jamison-Koenig, Zheng, "The Impact of Cognitive Load on the Cardiac Orienting Response to Auditory Structural Features during Natural Radio Listening Situations", Journal of Cognition 2018. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6634355/ (quotes are from the fetch tool's extraction of the page text; the 2015 habituation quotes are the paper's description of earlier work, not new data)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Partly. Within-person design compares repeated onsets of the same feature, and Study 2 manipulates task difficulty. The audio event itself is the manipulated variable. Content was not matched to a no-event control in this paper.

MUSIC IN SHORT VIDEO 1: an observational study of 12,842 Douyin videos finds an inverted-U between audio emotional arousal and engagement (abstract-only)

Abstract: "this study applies multimodal machine learning to analyze 12,842 short videos from Douyin, integrating text analysis, sound recognition, and image processing. The results reveal an inverted U-shaped relationship between auditory emotional arousal and consumer engagement, where moderate arousal maximizes interaction while excessively high or low arousal reduces engagement. Visual variation, however, exhibits a positive linear effect, with greater variation driving higher engagement. Notably, audiovisual congruence significantly enhances engagement, as high alignment between arousal and visual variation optimizes consumer information processing."

This is the only study found that measures audio features against engagement on real short videos. I could only read the abstract (the journal site returned 403). So I do not know: which engagement metric (likes, comments, shares, watch time), the effect sizes, what was controlled (follower count, topic, posting time), or how "visual variation" was operationalized (it may include cut rate). The audio arousal measure came from a sound-recognition model, so it mixes voice tone and music.

Treat as a correlation across videos made by different creators. It cannot tell the editor that raising or lowering arousal would change anything for the same video.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Yang, Wang, Wang, Jiang, Li, "Harmonizing Sight and Sound...", Journal of Theoretical and Applied Electronic Commerce Research 2025, abstract-only via Crossref API https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3390/jtaer20020069 ; page https://www.mdpi.com/0718-1876/20/2/69 (403)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? No, as far as the abstract shows. Observational, across different videos and creators. Audio is separated from visual variation statistically (both in one model), but not from creator, topic or content, as far as the abstract states.

MUSIC IN VIDEO 2: in a lab experiment, edit pace and music arousal were varied independently; both made time feel faster; editing did not change emotional involvement

Sam and Reddy (2023): 39 participants, 3x2 repeated-measures design, editing density (master shot, low, high) crossed with music arousal (high, low), eye-tracked. Abstract: "Fast-paced editing was associated with the feeling of time passing faster, a relationship mediated by fixation durations. High-arousal background music was also associated with the feeling of time passing faster."

Negative results from the text: "Editing density did not predict emotional involvement, in contrast with existing research". "There was also no interaction between editing density and arousal". "Fixation durations did not mediate the relationship between editing density and DJs [duration judgments] or emotional involvement." The authors' own limitation: "the sample size was small compared to previous studies on editing density and time perception. As a result, it had less statistical power and might not have detected some small effects." They also concede it "was not a true retrospective timing task".

The outcome is felt time passing, not attention or retention. The authors speculate that "music 'drives' the experience of time more than visual information like pace of editing", labelled as a possible auditory driving effect, not a tested result. High-arousal music also produced larger pupils (F(2,213)=123.6, p<.001).

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Sam and Reddy, "The Effect of Music and Editing Style on Subjective Perception of Time When Watching Videos: An Eye-tracking Study", Projections 17(2), 2023, doi 10.3167/proj.2023.170203; full PDF fetched from https://labocinemedias.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/The_Effect_of_Music_and_Editin.pdf
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Yes. Editing density and music arousal were crossed in one design, so each effect is estimated with the other held fixed. But the stimuli are a small set of lab clips and the outcomes are time-perception ratings.

SOUND ON VS SOUND OFF 1: same footage with sound vs without sound (subtitles on in both), 161 viewers

Szarkowska et al. (2024): participants watched two 6-minute clips from a Netflix series with English subtitles, "one video was presented with sound, the other without", counterbalanced. Reported: "participants had higher comprehension when they watched videos with sound (81.30%) than without (77.70%)". Cognitive load was higher without sound, and "participants reported enjoying the videos more with sound (5.52) than without sound (4.47)" on a 7-point scale. Sample: 161 (43 British, 53 Australian L1-English, 65 Polish L2-English). Eye-tracking was also collected.

Size of the effect: about 3.6 percentage points of comprehension, about 1 point of 7 on enjoyment. These are large enough to be significant in a within-person design, but small in practice for comprehension.

Not short-form: the clips were six minutes of drama dialogue, with subtitles on in both conditions. This answers "does a viewer who can hear lose anything", not "does adding sound raise watch time". The paper measures no retention or swipe-away behavior. The viewers were recruited and watched under study instruction, so it says nothing about what happens when feed viewers choose.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Szarkowska et al., "Watching subtitled videos with the sound off affects viewers' comprehension, cognitive load, immersion, enjoyment, and gaze patterns: A mixed-methods eye-tracking study", PLoS One 2024. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11458047/ (abstract-level numbers as returned by the fetch)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Yes. The sound track is the only manipulated variable. Same viewers, content-matched, order counterbalanced. Limits: not short-form, subtitled in both conditions, and this is not voice vs no voice (dialogue was present in the sound-on condition).

LOUDNESS 2: across 27,000 TV ads, more "energetic" ads were less likely to be tuned out, and the authors say the finding is about more than loudness

Yang, Xie, Krishnamurthi and Papatla (Journal of Marketing Research, 2022). Abstract: "Using a data set of over 27,000 TV commercials delivered to U.S. homes between 2015 and 2018... more energetic commercials are more likely to be tuned in or less likely to be avoided by viewers. The positive association between energy levels in ad content and ad tuning is statistically significant after controlling for placement and other aspects of commercials. However, the association varies across product categories and program genres. The main implication of this study is that advertisers should pay attention to components of ad content other than loudness, which has been regulated by law."

Energy is measured by an algorithm "from the video of ads" and "is related to the level of arousal stimulated by ad content". The University of Notre Dame release describes the audio energy feature as "a perceptual measure of intensity and powerful activity released throughout the track. Typical energetic tracks feel fast, loud, and noisy". It also notes the CALM Act limits ad loudness.

Limits: TV ads, not short video; outcome is viewers changing channel, not swiping; observational. I read the abstract and the press release only, so I do not know how much of the effect comes from the audio component alone as opposed to the visuals.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Yang, Xie, Krishnamurthi, Papatla, "High-Energy Ad Content: A Large-Scale Investigation of TV Commercials", Journal of Marketing Research 2022, abstract via Crossref API https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222437211067802 ; Notre Dame release https://news.nd.edu/news/loud-and-clear-high-energy-ads-keep-viewers-tuned-in-study-shows/
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Partly. Placement and other ad aspects were controlled statistically, but this is observational and not randomized. Audio energy and visual energy are measured together from the ad.

SPEECH RATE 1: fast speech raised persuasion in two field experiments (449 participants); abstract-only

Miller, Maruyama, Beaber and Valone (1976): "The relationship between speaking rate and attitude change was investigated in 2 field experiments with 449 Ss. Manipulations of speech rate were crossed with (a) credibility of the speaker and (b) complexity of the spoken message. Results suggest that speech rate functions as a general cue that augments credibility; rapid speech enhances persuasion, and therefore argues against information-processing interpretations of the effects of a fast speaking rate."

Persuasion here means attitude change toward a message, and the manipulated stimulus is a spoken message, not video. It says nothing about whether a faster voice keeps people watching. The abstract does not give the words-per-minute used or the effect sizes, and I could not fetch the full text. The authors conclude that perceived credibility, not better processing, explains the effect.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Miller, Maruyama, Beaber, Valone, "Speed of speech and persuasion", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 34(4), 1976, abstract-only: https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/speed-of-speech-and-persuasion
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Yes for speech rate. Rate was manipulated and crossed with speaker credibility and message complexity, so rate is separated from content. It is a 1976 study of spoken persuasion, not of edited video.

SPEECH RATE 2: a faster voice did not always persuade more; it cut both ways depending on whether the listener agreed

Smith and Shaffer (1991): participants "heard either a proattitudinal or a counterattitudinal message on an involving topic, delivered at a slow (144 words per minute), an intermediate (182 wpm), or a rapid (214 wpm) rate of speech." Result: "rapid speech suppressed the tendency to rebut the counterattitudinal message and enhanced persuasion, whereas the same rapid speech rate inhibited favorable elaboration of the proattitudinal message while undermining its persuasive impact. Thus, a distinctly faster than normal rate of speech on an involving topic can either promote or inhibit persuasion by its impact on message elaboration."

This is the negative half of the Miller result. The effect of speaking fast depended on whether the audience already agreed. The sample size is not in the abstract (abstract-only, via Crossref). The topic was "involving", so the result may not carry over to low-involvement entertainment video. Comprehension was not the outcome.

SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Smith and Shaffer, "Celerity and Cajolery: Rapid Speech May Promote or Inhibit Persuasion through its Impact on Message Elaboration", Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 17(6), 1991, abstract via Crossref API https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146167291176009 (journal page returned 403)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? Yes. Speech rate was randomly assigned at three levels, with message direction crossed. Audio-only, and persuasion is the outcome.

SPEECH RATE 3: in 6.9 million edX video sessions, faster-speaking instructors were watched longer, but the authors say speaking rate is a stand-in for enthusiasm

Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014) measured "how long students are watching each video, and whether they attempt to answer post-video assessment problems" across "6.9 million video watching sessions across four courses on the edX MOOC platform". Speaking rate was computed from subtitles: words divided by speaking time. "Speaking rates range from 48 to 254 words per minute (mean = 156 wpm, sd = 31 wpm)." "Within a particular length range, engagement usually increases (up to 2x) with speaking rate." But: "for 6-12 minute videos, engagement dips in the middle bucket (145-165 wpm); slower-speaking videos are more engaging than mid-speed ones." And: "speaking rate is merely a surface feature that correlates with enthusiasm and thus engagement. Thus, speeding up an unenthusiastic instructor might not improve engagement." They found "no significant differences in the numbers of play and pause events" across rates. They report that edX producers "tightly edit in post-production to remove instances of 'umm', 'uhh', filler words, and other pauses", but the paper does not test that practice.
