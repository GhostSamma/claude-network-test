JOB 2 - THE OPENING OF A VIDEO: WHAT IS MEASURED ABOUT THE FIRST SECONDS
Topic: does the opening (first ~3 seconds) decide watch-through, and has anyone measured which opening technique works
Date: 2026-10-03 (all sources fetched this session; Meta's own 3-second help page and the Nielsen original could not be fetched)
Number of sections: 21
Number of NOT FOUND sub-questions: 10 (listed in section 20)

SECTION 1. "Up to 47% of a video campaign's value is delivered in the first three seconds" - what the original says
Facebook's own page states: "Facebook found with Nielsen that up to 47% of the value in a video campaign was delivered in the first three seconds, while up to 74% of the value was delivered in the first ten." A trade site reproducing Facebook's post gives the method: "Then our marketing science team looked at both the lift data from Nielsen and the number of people who watched for varying lengths of time to estimate campaign value at different lengths of view." and "people who watched under three seconds of the video ad created up to 47 percent of the total campaign value". So "value" = lift x head-count (my reading of those two quotes). Most people leave early, so early-leavers add up to a big slice. It says nothing about what was in the opening or whether a better opening would have kept anyone.
Criticism relayed by a blog (attribution to Byron Sharp unverified; the blog says "Or someone purporting to be him"): "The 'uplifts' in this research are the comparison between those who chose to watch the ad to a similar group of people who were not exposed". The blog says Nielsen analyzed 173 BrandEffect studies. Nielsen's own report: NOT FOUND (only Facebook's summary and trade-press copies).
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Facebook "Capture Attention with Updated Features for Video Ads" https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads ; method text via https://www.smallscreenproducer.com/nielsen-study-the-value-of-video-ads-on-facebook/ and https://tenscores.com/daily/facebook-ads/facebook-publishes-nielsen-study-results-questionable-best-201503240700/
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Viewers chose how long to watch, so viewer interest in the product is mixed in with everything; the edit/opening content was never varied.

SECTION 2. The 47% number changed meaning as it was copied (who cites whom)
Origin: Facebook post on a Nielsen study, March 2015 (the blog Tenscores, dated 24 March 2015, is already criticizing it).
Link 1, Marketing Dive, 19 March 2015, turns "share of value" into a lift: "Viewers who watched less than three seconds experienced a 47% lift in ad recall, 32% in brand awareness, and 44% in purchase intent." Marketing Dive also adds that Facebook "counts a view as watching for three seconds".
Link 2, Vici Media (undated in fetched text), repeats the lift version: "Nielsen found that after just 3 seconds ad recall for the advertiser increased 47%, brand awareness increased 32%, and purchase intent increased 44%".
Link 3, Marketing Essentials Lab, turns Facebook's 65%/45% (section 4) into a rule: "it takes only three seconds for your viewers to decide if they'll continue to watch or scroll past your video", and garbles it: "45 per cent of those will watch for thirty seconds" (Facebook: 45% of all who watched three seconds).
SOURCE: [VENDOR] chain pages: https://www.marketingdive.com/news/brand-lift-happens-in-less-than-1-second-of-video-study-finds/377333/ ; https://www.vicimediainc.com/post/can-less-than-3-seconds-of-a-video-ad-have-an-impact ; https://marketingessentialslab.com/%E2%80%A8the-three-second-social-media-rule/
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. None of these pages contains an experiment; they are copies of one Facebook post.

SECTION 3. The chain continues into 'hook' blogs, which add claims with no source
Link 4, Brandefy: "nearly 65% of people who watch the first 3 seconds of a video will watch for at least 10 seconds", then "critical window"; its Facebook link now returns 404.
Link 5, Percee Digital: "2.4x more likely to land on the For You page, according to Brandefy and TikTok Creator Insights (2024)". The Brandefy page I fetched has no 2.4x. Percee: "We have tested this across dozens of campaigns", "consistently improves retention by 15 to 30 percent", no data shown.
Link 6, ROASPIG: "This isn't arbitrary - it's based on user behavior data and how Meta's algorithm evaluates video performance." Coinis: "Most viewers in the Facebook Feed decide within that window whether to keep watching or scroll right past." Neither gives a source.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] https://brandefy.com/psychology-of-viral-video-openers/ ; https://www.perceedigital.com/insights/video-hooks-that-convert/
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Marketing blogs; the 15-30% and 2.4x figures have no visible data or source.

SECTION 4. The 65% / 45% statistic is a survivor statistic, not a test of the opening
Facebook: "We know that 65% of people who watch the first three seconds of a video will watch for at least ten seconds and 45% continue watching for thirty seconds." This is conditional on already having watched three seconds. It does not say how many people left before three seconds, what they saw, or whether any change to the opening would shift the 65%. People who stay past three seconds are, by selection, more interested, so they are likely to stay longer whatever the edit. The paragraph goes on to suggestions ("Front-loading lifestyle/product shots and recognizable spokespeople can create interest, as can scenes with action or vivid backgrounds") with no test cited. The page's footnote for the 65% figure did not come through in the fetch and no underlying data is shown; Percee Digital calls it "Facebook/Meta internal data".
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Facebook "Capture Attention with Updated Features for Video Ads" https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Pure descriptive retention among self-selected viewers.

SECTION 5. Facebook used the "short exposures matter" number to answer a viewability complaint
Marketing Dive, 30 June 2017, reports agencies complaining that "viewability rates are as low as 20%" and Facebook's reply: "a recent Nielsen study showed that 38% of brand recall, 23% of brand awareness and 25% of purchase intent are driven by video impressions that are under two seconds." Note the cut-off here is two seconds, not three. In this use it defends ad delivery: a claim about short exposures, not about what an opening should contain.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Marketing Dive, "Facebook: Why mobile video ads must work fast" https://www.marketingdive.com/news/facebook-why-mobile-video-ads-must-work-fast/446217/
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Same Nielsen exposure-duration data as section 1; no variation in content.

SECTION 6. "3 seconds" is a metric threshold each platform picked; the platforms disagree
Meta: Marketing Dive (2015) says Facebook "counts a view as watching for three seconds". Meta's own help page for the 3-second metric is JavaScript-rendered and I could not fetch its text (NOT FOUND via fetch).
Instagram, as quoted by Social Media Today: "While view rate shows what percentage watched past the first 3 seconds of your Reel, skip rate is the percentage of views from people who decided to skip your reel during those first 3 seconds."
TikTok Ads Help: "Number of times your video starts playing for at least 2 seconds in an impression session." and "Number of times your video starts playing for at least 6 seconds in an impression session".
YouTube Help (Shorts): "How many times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops." - no number given. YouTube Help also says: "Beginning on 24 August 2026, views are counted the moment that a video starts to play across all formats, including Shorts".
Reading: the threshold is a reporting definition (2 s, 3 s, 6 s, or unstated). None of these pages says the number was chosen because research found decisions are made there. A hook-rate vendor admits: "There's no single industry number, since Meta doesn't publish one".
SOURCE: [PLATFORM DOC] https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/video-play ; https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en-GB&co=GENIE.Platform%3DDesktop ; Instagram text seen via https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-adds-retention-insights-reels/758464/ ; Meta via Marketing Dive link in section 2
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. These are metric definitions, not effects of anything.

SECTION 7. Has the three-second rule itself been tested? NOT FOUND
I searched for a randomized or controlled test that varies the opening and measures watch-through (arXiv/SSRN style queries; "hook rate" A/B test queries). Nothing found. What exists instead: vendors defining "hook rate" as a ratio, e.g. "Hook rate measures how many viewers watch past the first 3 seconds of your video." (Marpipe) and "Hook rate = (3-second video views ÷ impressions) × 100. Aim for 25% as a baseline" (adlibrary), with the claim that "Below 25%, rework the first frame." adlibrary's own text says the bands are "practitioner data"; no data shown. adlibrary asserts Meta's ranking system "reads that as low engagement quality and throttles delivery"; I did not find a Meta document stating this. The number is a metric plus a repeated blog claim, not a result.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] https://adlibrary.com/posts/hook-rate ; https://www.marpipe.com/ad-glossary/hook-rate
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. No study exists in what I could find, so nothing separates anything.

SECTION 8. TikTok's own "63% of top-CTR videos put the key message in the first 3 seconds" has no base rate
TikTok for Business PDF "9 Creative Tips": "Our research shows that over 63% of all videos with the highest click-through rate (CTR) highlight their key message or product within the first 3 seconds." Missing: what share of ALL videos (including weak ones) do the same. If 80% of all ads put the product up early, 63% would be below average. Same document: "33% auction ads with the highest VTR break the 4th wall" (look at camera), "40% of auction ads with the highest VTR have adopted this creative tip" (text overlay), and "TikToks shot vertically have an average 25% higher 6-second watch-through rate". Only the last has a comparison group, and it is about frame shape, not the opening. Sample size, ad count and dates: not given. Percee's "2.4x" (section 3) is not in this document.
SOURCE: [PLATFORM DOC] TikTok for Business, "9 Creative Tips to drive performance" https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Auction_Ads_Creative_Tips.pdf (TikTok sells these ads, so treat the numbers as vendor numbers)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Descriptive shares among top performers; product, brand and budget not controlled.

SECTION 9. Google/YouTube "The First 5 Seconds" (2015): the largest creative-attribute study, with no effect sizes published
YouTube Insights Team, June 2015: "we looked into thousands of TrueView ads across 16 countries and 11 verticals, categorizing them according to 170 creative attributes" and used "aggregated analytics from AdWords to see how long people watched without hitting the skip button". Findings, all in the first five seconds: "ads with the brand in the first five seconds have higher ad recall and brand awareness lift, people are also more likely to skip them"; "People are more likely to watch humorous ads"; "having a recognizable face in the first five seconds of your TrueView ad tends to result in higher viewership and better brand lift"; "featuring any music in the first five seconds may have a negative impact" on brand awareness; "People were more likely to skip ads that featured calming, relaxing, or action-oriented music." The document says "we did find that certain creative choices are associated with how long viewers watch or how well they remember ads" - association language. No coefficients, no counts, no controls listed. Google sells these ads.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Think with Google, "The First 5 Seconds: Creating YouTube Ads That Break Through in a Skippable World" https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/953/creating-youtube-ads-that-break-through-in-a-skippable-world.pdf
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Opening features were coded per ad and compared across ads, so brand, product and budget differences sit inside the comparison; one small test (Mountain Dew Kickstart: three ads, "one with a lighter brand touch in the first five seconds was skipped less on mobile") is a single brand, no numbers.

SECTION 10. Google's Honey Maid test: shorter cuts of the same story were skipped MORE (a negative result for "faster is better")
Unskippable Labs (Google with Mondelez and Droga5), Sept 2015 survey, published March 2016: three cuts of one ad family (15 s, 30 s, 2:17) run as TrueView. "The longer cuts were both watched more than the 15-second ad, with the 30-second ad the least skipped, and the 15-second ad the most skipped." "its VTR was 30% higher than that of the 15-second spot." Conclusion in the text: "making ads shorter doesn't get them more attention - it may get them even less." But "The 15-second ad was the only one to drive significant ad recall across all three cuts." Also: "only about 15% of viewers watched the longest video ad all the way through". One brand, one story, three edits; the cuts differ in length, pacing, product timing (6 s, 11 s, 1:17) and story, so length is not separated from structure. No sample size or significance test is in the document.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Think with Google, Unskippable Labs "In Video Advertising, Is Longer Stronger?" https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/2647/37587_Unskippable_Labs_ARTICLE_-_EN.pdf
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Same underlying story and brand across all three versions, so topic is held roughly constant; but several edit choices changed at once.

SECTION 11. Becker et al. 2022: early or prominent branding goes with MORE zapping; creativity with less (2,500 viewers, 1,315 TV spots)
Journal of Marketing abstract: "The results of the first study show that ad creativity is associated with less zapping, whereas a strong information focus and a prominent or early integration of branding elements are associated with more zapping." Data: "a data set reflecting the zapping behavior of over 2,500 German television viewers combined with advertising data and content information for 1,315 spots representing 308 brands from 96 categories." Second study: "irritation (determined by feeling, e.g., annoyed, offended, or overwhelmed), relative to enjoyment, acts as the central mechanism". The effects "differ significantly for products with a utilitarian (vs. hedonic) consumption purpose". Abstract gives no effect sizes (full text is paywalled; I could not fetch it). This is TV commercials, not short feeds.
SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Becker, Scholdra, Berkmann, Reinartz (2022), "The Effect of Content on Zapping in TV Advertising", Journal of Marketing, abstract via https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/00222429221105818 (DOI 10.1177/00222429221105818)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Observational, but the model includes brand and product-category terms (the abstract names utilitarian/hedonic and search/experience goods), so some topic is statistically adjusted; the abstract does not say which time window "early" means.

SECTION 12. Teixeira, Wedel and Pieters 2010: one of the few experiments that edits the same commercial and measures zapping moment by moment
Marketing Science abstract: "Data on avoidance through zapping, along with eye tracking on 31 commercials for nearly 2,000 participants, are used to calibrate the model." "New, simple metrics of attention dispersion are shown to strongly predict avoidance." "central on-screen brand positions, but not brand size, further promote commercial avoidance." "brand pulsing--while keeping total brand exposure constant--decreases commercial avoidance significantly." And: "Both numerical simulations and a controlled experiment using regular and edited commercials, respectively, provide evidence of the benefits of brand pulsing". That edited-versus-regular comparison is the closest design to isolating an editing change (brand timing) from topic. Teixeira's later Think with Google essay summarizes: "if it's too soon and too long, people are likely to disengage." I could not get the full text (paywalled), so no effect sizes or per-second hazard figures are quoted. TV ads, not feeds.
SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Teixeira, Wedel, Pieters (2010), "Moment-to-Moment Optimal Branding in TV Commercials: Preventing Avoidance by Pulsing", Marketing Science 29(5), abstract via https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1100.0567 ; essay https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/2710/1070-The-Attention-Economy-Download.pdf
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? yes for the edited-commercial experiment (same commercial, only branding timing changed); partly for the model (31 different commercials).

SECTION 13. Teixeira, Wedel and Pieters 2012: eye tracking plus zapping, second by second, for internet video ads
Journal of Marketing Research abstract: "In a controlled experiment, the authors assessed joy and surprise through automated facial expression detection for a sample of advertisements. They assessed concentration of attention through eye tracking and viewer retention by recording zapping behavior." "Surprise and joy effectively concentrate attention and retain viewers. However, importantly, the level rather than the velocity of surprise affects attention concentration most, whereas the velocity rather than the level of joy affects viewer retention most. The effect of joy is asymmetric, with higher gains for increases than losses for decreases." Relevance to an editor: what retains viewers is the TRAJECTORY of emotion over time (rising joy), not a fixed opening trick. The abstract names no "first seconds" effect and gives no effect sizes (paywalled). Teixeira's Think with Google essay (citing a separate HBR piece) describes a "rollercoaster" emotion technique, also without opening-specific numbers.
SOURCE: [PEER-REVIEWED] Teixeira, Wedel, Pieters (2012), "Emotion-Induced Engagement in Internet Video Advertisements", Journal of Marketing Research 49(2), abstract via https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1509/jmr.10.0207
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Controlled experiment with measured emotions and gaze per moment; the ads differ in content, so emotion trajectory is observed across ads, not assigned.

SECTION 14. Eye tracking on cuts: viewers re-orient after every cut (151 TV ads, ~20 viewers each) - a preprint
Ye and Wedel (arXiv, 28 May 2026): "We assess the model's performance on 151 video ads, each seen by about 20 viewers wile their eye movements were tracked". The ads are "151 TV video ads that aired between 2023 and 2024", about 30 seconds, "average scene length of 2 seconds". Findings: "in general scene cuts cause the entropy to jump up as viewers need to reorient themselves. That is, they cause a loss in viewers' attention that needs to be regained after each cut." One example ad "is predicted to struggle to maintain attention at the very start (S1, S2)". The paper cites older work that "viewers tend to reorient and fixate the center of the frame after a cut". Limits: it measures where eyes land (spread of gaze), not whether people keep watching; TV ads, lab setting; the numbers for the model are predictions. A "cut on the first frame" is not tested; a cut-rate effect on watch-through is not tested here.
SOURCE: [PREPRINT] Ye and Wedel (2026), "ViASNet: A Video Ad Saliency Network for Predicting Dynamic Saliency and Viewer Engagement", https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29302 (PDF https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.29302)
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Within an ad the topic is constant while cuts vary, so after-cut gaze changes are about the edit; across ads topic is not controlled, and no watch-through outcome is measured.

SECTION 15. Measured early exits in short-form: ~70% of viewings end before 20% of the video (50 people, 100 videos)
Yen et al. (arXiv, 2026): crowdsourced, "fifty people aged between 18 and 30", five sessions of twenty videos each. "Users are free to swipe to watch the next video at any time". Videos "4 to 81 seconds, with an average duration of 34.95 seconds". Result: "approximately 70% of viewing sessions end before reaching 20% of the video duration, highlighting a pronounced early abandonment phenomenon". 20% of ~35 s is about 7 s, so most exits happen in the first several seconds, but the paper reports a ratio, not per-second exits, and does not tie exits to anything in the video. A small, young, crowdsourced sample on a YouTube-Shorts-style web player. Dataset is public (https://nvduc.github.io/shortvideodataset per the paper) - usable offline by the requester, but I did not download it.
SOURCE: [PREPRINT] Vu Thi Hai Yen, Duc V. Nguyen, Cao Anh Minh Huy, Truong Thu Huong (2026), "Short-Form Video Viewing Behavior Analysis and Multi-Step Viewing Time Prediction", https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22663
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Videos vary by category only; no opening feature is analyzed.

SECTION 16. YouTube skippable ads: 35% of skips happen in under 6 seconds, regardless of ad length (preprint, 2016 campus traces)
Arantes, Figueiredo, Almeida: "in more than one third (35%) of the cases, users skip the video-ad exhibition in less than 6 seconds (one second above the minimum)". "on average, a user skips a video-ad after only 20% of its content has been exhibited". "many video-ad exhibitions are largely ignored by the users, who skip them as early as they are allowed to, regardless of their durations." Also "in 29% of the video-ad exhibitions, users chose not to skip it", ranging "in the range of 17% (e.g., Music) to 49% (Comedy)" by category. Data come from a university campus network HTTP trace (2016 preprint; whether it was later published I could not verify from the fetched text). The 5-second pile-up is partly produced by the skip button appearing at 5 s, so this shows when people CAN skip, not that the opening content caused it.
SOURCE: [PREPRINT] Arantes, Figueiredo, Almeida (2016), "Understanding Video-Ad Consumption on YouTube: A Measurement Study on User Behavior, Popularity, and Content Properties", https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07890
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. The only content split is by ad category (Music 17% vs Comedy 49% completion), which is topic, not edit.

SECTION 17. Wistia's "nose": the first 2% of a video loses 4.9% (1-2 min videos) to 17.3% (5-10 min), and the loss scales with length
Wistia: "In this analysis, we define the nose as the first 2% of your video." "the average engagement drop in the nose is 4.9% for videos 1 - 2 minutes in length. That engagement loss increases to 17.3% for videos 5 - 10 minutes in length." The text says the nose loss is "highly correlated with video length", and "There's also way more pressure on the nose of a longer video." Notes for the requester: (a) these viewers had already clicked play on a business web page, a different situation from a feed; (b) a 2% window on a 90-second video is under two seconds, so for short clips the first-seconds loss in Wistia's data is small; (c) the listed remedies ("Experiment with removing intro graphics and logo sequences", "Hook your audience by showing and proving your point") are advice with no test cited in the page. Wistia sells video hosting.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Wistia, "Understanding Audience Retention" https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/understanding-audience-retention
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. Large-sample average across Wistia-hosted videos; no content variable beyond length.

SECTION 18. YouTube Help says opening retention is entangled with the title and thumbnail
YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention": "Intro tells you what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds." "A high intro percentage could mean: The content in the first 30 seconds matched the viewer's expectation of the video's thumbnail and title. The content kept the audience interested." Recommendations: "Consider changing your video thumbnail and title to better reflect your video content." and "Modify the first 30 seconds of your video and experiment with different styles". Two points: YouTube's own definition of the opening for long-form is 30 seconds, not 3; and the platform itself lists title/thumbnail match as a cause of opening retention, which is the topic-and-packaging effect the requester wants separated from the edit. No numbers given.
SOURCE: [PLATFORM DOC] YouTube Help https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? no. The doc says the metric mixes the two.

SECTION 19. Facebook IQ 2016 creative study: 19 rated features, regression on Ad Recall, nothing specific about the opening
Facebook IQ, 20 April 2016: raters (people "who did not have a creative or advertising background") "first watched the first 10 seconds of the video ad with sound turned off" then 30 seconds with sound, answering "19 different questions grouped within four creative dimensions: first impressions, branding, messaging and video features". Scores for "over 600 video ads" were matched to "over 200 Nielsen Brand Effect studies" and a linear regression "was able to account for 82% of the variation in Ad Recall scores (r2=0.82)". Reported conclusion: "the strongest Ad Recall scores occurred when video ad creative showcased the products or services, included conversations and brand links and got the message across succinctly". No coefficient for any first-seconds feature is published, even though a "first impressions" dimension was coded. Outcome is ad recall, not watch-through. Facebook also says "Internal tests show that captioned video ads increase video view time by an average of 12%" - internal, unpublished, caption (on-screen text) rather than opening. A Facebook case study (Seda Sunsilk) mentions a question opener ("No hairstyle ideas for a night out?") alongside a "7 point lift in unaided brand awareness", but the lift cannot be credited to the question.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Facebook IQ, "Capturing Attention in Feed: The Science Behind Effective Video Creative" https://www.facebook.com/business/news/insights/capturing-attention-feed-video-creative ; captions and case study text at https://www.facebook.com/business/news/updated-features-for-video-ads
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Ads were rated on creative features and the regression was run "while controlling for campaign characteristics", but ratings are subjective and the r2 of 0.82 on 600 ads with 19 predictors is not validated out of sample in the text.

SECTION 20. Scorecard: named opening techniques and what was actually measured
Brand or logo in the opening: measured, observational, and it cuts against the tip. Google 2015 (ads with the brand early had higher recall but "people are also more likely to skip them"), Becker 2022 (early branding goes with more zapping), Teixeira 2010 (edited-commercial experiment; pulsing reduces avoidance). Facebook, by contrast, advises "Incorporate your brand message and identity early" (Facebook 'Want to Make Better Video Ads for Mobile', no test cited). Recognizable face / smiling character in the first five seconds: association only (Google 2015). No music in the first five seconds: association for brand awareness only (Google 2015). Question opener: only a vendor case study, no control. Fourth-wall break / look at camera: TikTok says 33% of top-VTR ads do it, no base rate. Text overlay: TikTok 40% of top-VTR ads, no base rate; Facebook "internal tests" of captions +12% view time.
NOT FOUND sub-questions (10): (1) Nielsen's original report for the 47% figure; (2) any randomized or controlled test of the three-second rule; (3) controlled test of a cold open; (4) controlled test of a question opener; (5) controlled test of motion toward camera; (6) controlled test of a cut on the first frame; (7) eye-tracking study specifically on the first seconds of vertical short-form feed video; (8) the source of the "2.4x For You page" claim; (9) Meta's own help-page text for the 3-second metric (page not fetchable); (10) effect sizes for Google's 170-attribute study.
SOURCE: [VENDOR] Google https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/953/creating-youtube-ads-that-break-through-in-a-skippable-world.pdf ; Facebook https://www.facebook.com/business/news/want-to-better-video-ads-for-mobile-well-show-you-how ; TikTok https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Auction_Ads_Creative_Tips.pdf
SEPARATED FROM TOPIC? partly. Only Teixeira 2010's edited-commercial experiment changes one editing choice on the same commercial; everything else listed is cross-ad association.

SECTION 21. Verdict
The "first three seconds" rule is mostly folklore. The number everyone repeats traces to one 2015-2016 Facebook marketing post about a Nielsen study, and that post is about HOW MANY PEOPLE watch for how long, not about what is in the opening. Three seconds is also the threshold Meta and Instagram chose for a metric, which is a likely second source of the folklore. I found no randomized test of the rule, and no controlled test of any named opening technique (cold open, question, motion toward camera, cut on first frame). The measured material that does exist is about TV and online ads, is mostly observational, and points the other way on one popular tip: putting the brand or logo early goes with MORE skipping and zapping.
